Lesson 3-1Writing ratiosA ratio compares two amounts, and the order matters. 3 cups of apple juice to 2 cups of sparkling water is 3:2 — part to part. Apple juice to the whole drink is 3:5 — part to whole.
Lesson 3-1 · Part 2Equivalent ratios & scale factorMultiply both quantities by the same number and the comparison does not change. 1:3 scaled by ×4 is 4:12 — the recipe still tastes the same.
Lesson 3-2RatesA rate compares two amounts with different units, like miles and hours. "150 miles per hour" means 150 miles for every 1 hour.
Lesson 3-2 · Parts 2 & 3Unit rates & better buysPut the cost on top and the amount on the bottom, divide Top ÷ Bottom, drop the 1, and compare. The lower unit rate is the better value — even when its total price is higher.
👀 What to listen for
  • "Which amount is named first?" — the order of a ratio is part of the answer.
  • Scaling multiplies. Adding the same number to both parts is the most common slip: from 1:3, adding 3 to each gives 4:6, but the answer is 4:12.
  • "Per" means "for each one." A rate always names two different units.
  • The cost goes on top. Flipping the fraction gives items per dollar instead of dollars per item.
  • The smaller total price is not automatically the better buy.
🤝 How to help
  • Let your student write the ratio or set up the fraction first, before you say anything.
  • Ask them to explain why a step works, not just what the answer is.
  • If they are stuck, press the ? or Hint button on the question rather than giving the answer.
  • You do not need to know the math. Asking "how did you get that?" is the whole job.
  • Ten focused minutes beats an hour of frustration. Stop and tell me what was hard.

Part 1 · Writing Ratios

A ratio compares two amounts, and the order you write them in matters. Comparing one part to another part is part-to-part. Comparing a part to the total is part-to-whole.

6 ratios • two tries each
Remember how
Chef Reyes's drink uses 3 cups of apple juice for every 2 cups of sparkling water.
Part to part
3 : 2apple juice to sparkling water
→
Part to whole
3 : 5apple juice to the whole drink (3 + 2 = 5)
Order matters: water to apple juice is 2:3 — a different ratio. Always write the amount that is named first first.

Part 2 · Equivalent Ratios & Scale Factor

Find what the first quantity was multiplied by — that number is the scale factor — then multiply the second quantity by the same number.

5 builders • find the scale factor first
Remember how
Tahira's salad dressing uses 1 spoon of vinegar for every 3 spoons of olive oil. She wants to use 4 spoons of vinegar.
Find the scale factor
1 × 4 = 4the vinegar went from 1 to 4
→
Use the same factor
3 × 4 = 121 : 3 becomes 4 : 12
Watch out: scaling multiplies — it never adds. From 1 : 3, adding 3 to each part gives 4 : 6, but the correct equivalent ratio is 4 : 12. Check your answer by dividing back: 4 : 12 ÷ 4 = 1 : 3. ✓

Part 3 · Rates

A rate compares two amounts with different units, like miles and hours. Build a model by counting up by the rate, keeping the units lined up.

4 models • fill the table, then answer
Remember how
A high-speed train travels 150 miles per hour. The trip from Boston to Washington, D.C. is 450 miles. How long does the trip take?
Worked example: miles travelled after each hour at 150 miles per hour
Miles150300450
Hours123
The model lands on 450 miles at 3 hours, so the trip takes 3 hours. Division says the same thing: put the total distance on TOP (450) and the rate on the BOTTOM (150). 450 ÷ 150 = 3 hours.

Part 4 · Unit Rates

Set up the ratio yourself: cost on top, amount on the bottom. Divide both parts by the bottom number so the bottom becomes 1. Then drop the 1 and say the unit rate.

5 problems • build it, then solve it
Remember how
Theater A charges $15 for 3 tickets. What is the cost per ticket?
1 · Set up the ratio
$153 tickets
→
2 · Divide both parts by 3
$51 ticket
3 · Drop the 1. The division gives 5/1, but we do not keep the 1 — we write $5 per ticket. Careful: if you flip the fraction to 3/15, you get tickets per dollar instead of dollars per ticket.

Part 5 · Best Value

You cannot compare two packages by their total price, because they hold different amounts. Find each unit price first, then compare.

5 matchups • type both unit prices
Remember how
Bag A holds 4 lb for $7.00. Bag B holds 6 lb for $9.60. Which is the better buy?
Bag A · 7 ÷ 4
$1.75per pound
vs
Bag B · 9.60 ÷ 6
$1.60per pound
$1.60 is less, so Bag B is the better buy — even though $9.60 is the bigger total price. The lower unit price always wins when the products are the same.
