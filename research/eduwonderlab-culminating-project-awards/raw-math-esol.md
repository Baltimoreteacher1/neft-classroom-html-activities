# Raw Research: Mathematics and Multilingual/ESOL Pedagogy — EduWonderLab Culminating Projects

**Date checked:** 2026-07-14
**Scope:** Grade 6 culminating-project collection at <https://eduwonderlab.com/curriculum/> and representative project pages from Units 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10; primary/authoritative guidance for mathematics, multilingual learning, accessibility, culturally sustaining practice, assessment, project-based learning, and student agency.

## Findings

### Current baseline

The collection already has unusually good foundations:

- Ten unit project hubs offer two or three parallel contexts while holding the assessed mathematics and rubric constant. This is meaningful student choice.
- Representative projects ask students to research real quantities, calculate, compare with a peer, write a recommendation, and produce a final artifact. They include autosave, progress indicators, calculators/feedback, optional sentence starters, a self-check, print support, and a four-point rubric.
- Some pages include Spanish interface strings and writing frames. The site uses Atkinson Hyperlegible and generally clear student-facing language.
- Contexts span food, sports, design, shopping, engineering, technology, geography, and architecture. Some invite family/community knowledge.

These features align with parts of NCTM, CAST UDL 3.0, WIDA, and PBLWorks. The main award-level opportunity is moving from a polished self-guided simulation to a **documented cycle of consequential inquiry, mathematical modeling, multilingual discourse, critique/revision, public contribution, and measured impact**.

### What exemplary mathematics requires

1. **Rich tasks must make reasoning visible, not only produce correct calculations.** NCTM's eight Mathematics Teaching Practices call for meaningful goals, reasoning/problem-solving tasks, connected representations, discourse, purposeful questions, conceptual-to-procedural fluency, productive struggle, and eliciting/using student thinking. Award-level versions should collect multiple strategies, representations, assumptions, validation, and revision.
   Source: NCTM, *Principles to Actions*: <https://www.nctm.org/uploadedFiles/Standards_and_Positions/PtAExecutiveSummary.pdf>

2. **The mathematical practices should be assessed explicitly.** The Common Core Standards for Mathematical Practice include sense-making/perseverance, quantitative reasoning, argument/critique, modeling, strategic tool use, precision, structure, and repeated reasoning. A standards list containing only content codes understates the intellectual ambition.
   Source: <https://corestandards.org/mathematics-standards/> and <https://corestandards.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Math_Standards1.pdf>

3. **TRU supplies an award-facing quality lens.** Robust environments provide rich disciplinary content; productive struggle; equitable access; agency, ownership, and positive mathematical identity; and responsiveness to student thinking. Its observation tools turn these into “look-fors,” showing that the innovation changes student experience rather than merely adding attractive software.
   Sources: <https://truframework.org/dimensions/>; <https://truframework.org/tru-observation-guide/>; <https://truframework.org/publications/>

4. **Formative assessment must lead to action and revision.** NCTM describes clarifying targets, eliciting reasoning/misconceptions, feedback students can use, adjusting instruction, and self-reflection. One-shot correctness feedback is not the full cycle.
   Source: <https://www.nctm.org/uploadedFiles/Conferences_and_Professional_Development/Annual_Meeting_and_Exposition/Formative%20Assessment%20position%20statement.pdf>

5. **High expectations and differentiated access must coexist.** NCTM calls for responsiveness to students' backgrounds, experiences, cultural perspectives, traditions, and knowledge, plus access to challenging curriculum and differentiated support. Differentiation should broaden participation in the same important mathematics, not lower cognitive demand.
   Source: <https://www.nctm.org/Standards-and-Positions/Position-Statements/Access-and-Equity-in-Mathematics-Education/>

### What exemplary multilingual mathematics requires

6. **Content and language goals must be integrated.** WIDA's 2020 framework connects language development to disciplinary learning and treats multilingualism as an asset. Its four components are Standards Statements, Key Language Uses, Language Expectations, and Proficiency Level Descriptors. For Grade 6 mathematics, the central targets are not generic vocabulary; they are explaining and arguing mathematically.
   Sources: <https://wida.wisc.edu/teach/standards/eld>; <https://wida.wisc.edu/sites/default/files/resource/WIDA-ELD-Standards-Framework-2020-Edition-Grades-6-8.pdf>

7. **WIDA specifies Grade 6–8 mathematical-language performances.** Students should interpret and construct explanations by analyzing representations/solution paths, sharing solutions, describing data or strategies, and stating reasoning. They should create conjectures, generalize across cases, justify conclusions with evidence/facts, and evaluate peers' arguments. WIDA names tables, diagrams, simulations, graphs, manipulatives, if/then language, causal connectors, comparisons, and clarification questions as meaning-making resources.
   Source: WIDA Grades 6–8, pp. 37–40: <https://wida.wisc.edu/sites/default/files/resource/WIDA-ELD-Standards-Framework-2020-Edition-Grades-6-8.pdf>

8. **Interaction should co-construct understanding, not merely divide labor.** WIDA's 2025-updated interactive-learning bulletin says interaction supports content knowledge, language, belonging, and student production of knowledge. It recommends tasks requiring students to figure something out together; discourse moves; multimodal claims using text, data, diagrams, symbols, and expressions; reflection; and iterative cycles of expressing and co-constructing ideas. The current partner-comparison prompts are a start but generally do not create mathematical interdependence.
   Source: <https://wida.wisc.edu/sites/default/files/resource/FocusBulletin-Interactive-Learning-Multilingual-Learners-Content-Area-Classrooms.pdf>

9. **Scaffolding should be contingent and gradually released.** WIDA distinguishes planned macro-scaffolding from responsive micro-scaffolding and shows students using graphics, symbols, gestures, visuals, and language while engaging in challenging grade-level mathematics. A “Need a starter?” box is helpful but is not a full proficiency-responsive pathway.
   Source: <https://wida.wisc.edu/resources/FB/scaffolding-learning-multilingual-students-math> and <https://wida.wisc.edu/sites/default/files/resource/FocusBulletin-Scaffolding-Learning-Multilingual-Students-Math.pdf>

10. **Students should use their full linguistic repertoires.** WIDA defines translanguaging as using all available languages and varieties to communicate and make meaning. Projects should permit home-language research, planning, annotations, peer rehearsal, captions, and family interviews, with an English bridge where appropriate.
    Source: <https://wida.wisc.edu/resources/FB/translanguaging>

11. **Language growth can be documented through project portfolios.** WIDA recommends authentic project artifacts, student-led conferences, and analysis at discourse, sentence, and word/phrase dimensions, anchored to Key Language Uses and PLDs. This is a ready-made approach for showing mathematics achievement and language growth across ten projects.
    Source: <https://wida.wisc.edu/sites/default/files/resource/FocusBulletin-Supporting-Multilingual-Learners-Language-Growth-Through-Language-Development-Portfolios.pdf>

12. **Sentence support should develop reasoning rather than become a fill-in-the-blank ceiling.** The Writing Revolution includes because–but–so, subordinating conjunctions, transitions, appositives, sentence combining, and structured planning embedded in content. A TWR progression can move from oral rehearsal and because/but/so to claim–evidence–reasoning and a polished public explanation; students should be able to dismiss frames and compose freely.
    Sources: <https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/resources/book-resources/chapter-4/>; <https://www.thewritingrevolution.org/about/the-book/>

### What makes the design award-competitive

13. **A polished online activity is not yet full project-based learning.** PBLWorks' Gold Standard model includes a challenging problem/question, sustained inquiry, authenticity, student voice and choice, reflection, critique/revision, and a public product. The current projects are authentic-feeling and choice-rich, but many appear bounded by predetermined steps, a teacher-only audience, and one final submission.
    Sources: <https://www.pblworks.org/what-is-pbl/gold-standard-project-design>; Grade 6 mathematics/HQIM example: <https://www.pblworks.org/sites/default/files/2025-02/Gold%20Standard%20Project%20Based%20Learning%20designed%20as%20HQIM_2025%20%20.pdf>

14. **Performance assessment is compelling when it demonstrates deeper learning and reliable implementation.** Learning Policy Institute identifies content mastery, critical thinking/complex problem solving, collaboration, communication, learning how to learn, and academic mindsets; it also emphasizes tasks, student work, rubrics, reflection/improvement, and educator calibration.
    Sources: <https://learningpolicyinstitute.org/product/cpac-performance-assessments-support-student-learning-brief>; <https://learningpolicyinstitute.org/product/performance-assessments-college-admission>

15. **Forward-thinking means agency plus responsible action, not merely new technology.** OECD frames agency as setting a goal, reflecting, and acting responsibly to effect change, with co-agency among students, teachers, families, and community. A project becomes future-facing when a real stakeholder can use the result and students can see what changed.
    Source: <https://www.oecd.org/en/data/tools/oecd-learning-compass-2030.html>

16. **Cultural relevance should change whose knowledge and problems count.** New York State's CR-S framework calls for affirming racial, linguistic, and cultural identities; rigorous independent learning; connection across difference; elevating marginalized voices; and agency for social change. Swapping names, flags, or foods is representation; co-defining a local problem with families/community is culturally sustaining design.
    Source: <https://www.nysed.gov/crs/framework>

17. **Accessibility must be verified, not inferred from attractive design.** CAST UDL 3.0 emphasizes learner agency; authentic choice; belonging; action-oriented feedback; multiple ways to perceive, make meaning, and communicate; support for math notation and multilingualism; and accessible technology. WCAG 2.2 AA includes visible/unobscured focus, adequate target size, consistent help, non-color cues, error identification, reflow, and keyboard access.
    Sources: <https://udlguidelines.cast.org/>; <https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/>; <https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/>

## Recommended upgrades

Upgrades are ordered by likely award leverage. Each includes observable evidence for an application.

### Tier 1 — Transform the learning model

1. **Give every unit a consequential local driving question and real client.** Convert the fictional brief into a real decision: design a lower-waste celebration; compare grocery unit prices for a family resource; map an accessible event; optimize a package for a local maker; model aquarium volume/cost for a classroom. Students interview a client, identify constraints, and deliver a recommendation the client can accept, adapt, or reject.
   **Evidence:** client brief/video, student-generated questions, constraints log, final recommendation, client feedback, record of a real decision.

2. **Add a mathematical modeling cycle to all projects.** Make visible: define problem → identify quantities/constraints → choose assumptions → represent/model → calculate → test against data/extreme cases → revise → communicate limitations. Require at least two representations and “Where could this model fail?”
   **Evidence:** model version history, representations, assumption/limitation logs, SMP 2/4/5/6 rubric scores, revisions after validation.

3. **Build a required critique–revision–defense loop.** Replace the single partner-comparison prompt with a private draft; peer critique using “notice / question / challenge”; revision memo; and short defense to a peer, family member, or expert. Preserve individual mathematical accountability.
   **Evidence:** first/final drafts, peer comments, revision memo, 60–90 second defense, discourse transcript, calibrated rubric.

4. **Publish to an authentic but privacy-safe audience.** Use opt-in classroom exhibitions, family math nights, school/client briefs, printed galleries, or moderated anonymous showcases. Never require names, faces, precise location, personal finances, or public accounts. Keep educator solutions out of student-facing routes and shipped client code.
   **Evidence:** exhibition program, anonymized gallery, audience feedback, privacy checklist/consent protocol, accessibility statement.

5. **Turn choice into student-authored inquiry.** Keep the polished pathways but add “Design Your Own Context” with a proposal checkpoint and common success criteria. Let students choose stakeholder, setting, data source, tool, medium, and language(s), while content requirements remain invariant.
   **Evidence:** proposals, context/media/language distribution, approval criteria, completion/quality comparisons across choices.

6. **Use trustworthy current data and teach provenance.** Provide a safe source bank plus an open research route. Require title/URL/date, units, credibility check, and note on uncertainty/bias. Seed conflicting data so students must decide rather than copy. Avoid personal financial/location data.
   **Evidence:** source logs, annotated datasets, credibility judgments, revisions prompted by data quality.

### Tier 2 — Make multilingual mathematics a signature innovation

7. **Publish paired content and WIDA language targets on every project.** Identify ELD-MA.6-8.Explain and/or ELD-MA.6-8.Argue beside math standards, with “I can” goals: describe a strategy with a table/diagram/equation; justify using mathematical facts; ask for clarification; critique respectfully. Keep math and language rubric rows separate so English proficiency does not erase mathematical competence.
   **Evidence:** ten-unit target map, dual rubric, ML/non-ML work samples, score-distribution/bias review.

8. **Create graduated, dismissible support layers.** Offer without ability labels: visual worked-example fragments; illustrated bilingual glossary with symbols/cognates; audio directions; “say it first” rehearsal; word/phrase bank; sentence starters; paragraph organizer; models at two proficiency bands; and unrestricted response. Fade supports across drafts.
   **Evidence:** nonidentifying support-use telemetry, before/after samples, student dignity/usefulness surveys, responsive-scaffolding notes.

9. **Make translanguaging an explicit right and resource.** Let students research/interview in a home language; use bilingual notes/diagram labels; discuss multilingually; create a bilingual final product or English summary with home-language companion; and receive family audio feedback. Human-review machine translations.
   **Evidence:** multilingual artifacts, family contributions, language-choice reflections, participation/completion data by ML status.

10. **Engineer mathematical interdependence and equitable talk.** Give partners complementary data, constraints, or models so neither finishes alone. Rotate roles (modeler, skeptic, connector, reporter), provide WIDA discourse moves, private think time, and an individual post-collaboration explanation. Do not grade accent, speed, or spontaneous fluency.
    **Evidence:** participation maps, discourse samples coded for explaining/arguing/critique, role logs, individual transfer checks.

11. **Embed a TWR-to-WIDA reasoning ladder.** Start with content-specific because–but–so or if–then; then sentence combining/expansion; then claim–evidence–reasoning; finally an audience-aware brief with frames hidden by default. Example: “This plan is proportional because… / but… / so…” Reward mathematical relationships, not length.
    **Evidence:** sentence-to-paragraph progression, rubric separating reasoning from conventions, independent transfer without frames.

12. **Build a longitudinal math-language portfolio.** Save one explanation, argument/critique, visual/model, reflection, feedback, and revision per project. In student-led conferences, students select growth evidence and set content/language goals using age-appropriate WIDA-aligned descriptors.
    **Evidence:** anonymized portfolio exemplars, discourse/sentence/word-phrase growth rubric, conference protocol, longitudinal chart.

### Tier 3 — Strengthen rigor, inclusion, and proof

13. **Replace answer checking with diagnostic feedback.** Diagnose a likely misconception or representation mismatch, ask a purposeful question, offer an optional hint, then require a corrected explanation. Give teachers an anonymous misconception dashboard. Preserve productive struggle; do not reveal solutions after repeated attempts.
    **Evidence:** misconception taxonomy, feedback rules, revision frequency, pre/post misconception rates, teacher response log.

14. **Create a common quality framework across ten units.** Map each project to content standards, SMPs, TRU, WIDA, UDL, and shared success skills. Use common anchor papers and calibration so “4” means the same thing across units/classes. Include an individual math checkpoint with any team product.
    **Evidence:** crosswalk, assessment map, anchors, calibration protocol, inter-rater agreement, reliability review.

15. **Audit contexts with students and community members.** Use a compensated student advisory board to check authenticity, cultural assumptions, stereotypes, family accessibility, and whether contexts invite local knowledge. Offer equivalent alternatives when food costs, bedrooms, family finances, or sports could expose personal circumstances.
    **Evidence:** advisory notes, “you said / we changed” log, context audit rubric, demographic participation analysis.

16. **Design to UDL 3.0 and verify WCAG 2.2 AA end to end.** Add text/audio/visual directions, low-bandwidth/print modes, keyboard operation, semantic labels, screen-reader-readable math, non-color cues, visible focus, 200–400% reflow, reduced motion, accessible error recovery, 24×24 px minimum targets, and equivalents to drag/canvas. Keep directions short, numbered, plain-language, and bilingual.
    **Evidence:** automated/manual audit, keyboard/screen-reader script, usability tests including disabled/ML learners, remediation log.

17. **Measure equity and impact, not just satisfaction.** Pilot transparently using matched pre/post content plus a transfer task; score math reasoning and language separately; track completion, revision, agency/belonging, and access by ML status, proficiency, disability, and other locally appropriate groups. Suppress small cells, minimize collection, and report null/negative results.
    **Evidence:** pre-registered questions, de-identified codebook, rubric reliability, effect sizes/uncertainty, opportunity-to-learn analysis, iteration log.

18. **Document teacher learning and replicability.** Package a 15-minute launch guide, standards/assessment map, facilitation moves, anticipated strategies/misconceptions, ML scaffolding guide, accessibility checklist, exemplars, and calibration video. Pilot beyond the designer's class and publish adaptations and failure points.
    **Evidence:** external-teacher pilot, adaptation logs, interviews, implementation time/cost, open sample unit and case study.

## Award-positioning by category

| Award frame | Strongest upgrades | Proof judges will expect |
|---|---|---|
| Mathematics teaching | Modeling cycle; representations; argument/critique; diagnostic feedback; calibrated rubric | Transfer growth, reasoning artifacts, misconception reduction, scoring reliability |
| Multilingual/ESOL mathematics | Paired WIDA targets; translanguaging; multimodal scaffolding; co-constructed discourse; separate math/language scoring; portfolio | Work across proficiency levels, discourse evidence, equitable participation, math growth not suppressed by English level |
| Education innovation | Real client; student-authored inquiry; revision; public contribution; accessible platform | Adoption, client use, before/after artifacts, student/teacher voice, accessibility verification |
| Forward-thinking/future-ready | Data provenance; uncertainty; ethical modeling; agency/co-agency; community action; privacy-safe portfolios | Real outcomes, limitations/ethics reasoning, community feedback, privacy safeguards |

## Gaps

- This was a web review, not a classroom observation. Offline facilitation, critique, exhibitions, family involvement, and language practices may be stronger than the pages show.
- Representative student pages from Units 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10 were inspected; every state in every version was not exhaustively tested.
- No student work, rubric-score data, multilingual-proficiency data, analytics, interviews, or accessibility conformance report was available. Learning-impact claims cannot yet be made.
- Public landing pages include links labeled for educator solutions. Access controls and contents were not tested. Before an award submission, verify instructional solutions are not retrievable from student routes, source, shipped JavaScript, or predictable URLs.
- Spanish coverage appears inconsistent across sampled projects, and translation quality was not evaluated by a qualified bilingual educator. Do not claim full-language parity without human review.
- Visible local standards labels (for example Maryland 6.AT codes) were not audited task-by-task for complete alignment or depth.
- Award criteria vary and change. These upgrades strengthen credible submissions but cannot guarantee a win.
