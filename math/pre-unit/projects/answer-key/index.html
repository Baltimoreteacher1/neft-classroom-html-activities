Pre-Unit Project — Teacher Answer Key

Worked solutions for Launch Day Supply Depot (Version A). Level 1 defaults are shown first; Level 0 defaults follow. Level 2 has no fixed answer — score the design against the four constraints.

Pre-Unit · Division 6.NOS.1 6.NOS.2 6.NOS.3 Teacher Use Only

What this project is testing. Not whether a student can divide — the five Pre-Unit lessons already did that separately. It is testing whether the student recognises the same question (how many fit?) across whole numbers, decimals and fractions, and whether they can say what a quotient means once it stops being a number on a page.

The one thing to look for. A student who writes 30 buses has divided correctly and answered the wrong question. The remainder is the assessment.

Part 1 · Whole-number division — Lesson 2-6 · 6.NOS.2

Load the Buses

Students riding (Level 1 default)
738
Seats on one bus
24
31 buses
738 ÷ 24 = 30 remainder 18.
30 buses fill completely; 18 students are still waiting.
Those 18 students need seats, so the order is 30 + 1 = 31 buses.
Level 0 default
100 ÷ 24
Level 0 answer
5 buses
Sample response — "What does the leftover mean?"

24 riders fit on each bus, but 18 students were left over, so we need one more bus even though it will be almost empty.

Common wrong answers and what they tell you

30 — dropped the remainder. The page names this ("that is the number of buses that fill up completely") without stating 31.
30.75 — treated a count of buses as a continuous quantity.
17,712 — multiplied.

Part 1 · Decimal division — Lesson 2-7 · 6.NOS.3

Buy the Supply Packs

Money set aside (Level 1 default)
$58.50
Price of one pack
$2.25
26 packs
Step A — equivalent division: 58.50 ÷ 2.25 = 5850 ÷ 225.
Both numbers were multiplied by 100, so the quotient is unchanged.
Step B — 5850 ÷ 225 = 26 exactly, with nothing left over.
Level 0 default
$4.50 ÷ $1.50
Level 0 answer
3 packs (45 ÷ 15)
Common wrong answers

2600 — moved the decimal point in the dividend only. The hint ladder names exactly this.
131.63 — multiplied instead of dividing.
Step A accepted in any equivalent form (5850 ÷ 225, 585 ÷ 22.5 is rejected because the divisor must be whole).

4
Part 2 · Fraction and mixed-number division — Lessons 6-1 and 6-2 · 6.NOS.1

Ribbon and Trail Mix

4a — ribbon on the roll
12 m
4a — ribbon per banner
3/4 m
16 banners
12 ÷ 3/4 = 12 × 4/3 = 48/3 = 16.
Sense-check: each piece is under a metre, so more than 12 pieces must fit. This is the 6-1 "how many fit" model with a whole-number dividend.
4b — trail mix in the bin
7 1/2 lb
4b — trail mix per bag
1 1/4 lb
6 bags
7 1/2 ÷ 1 1/4 = 15/2 ÷ 5/4 = 15/2 × 4/5 = 60/10 = 6 exactly.
Level 0 defaults: 6 ÷ 1/2 = 12 banners and 3 ÷ 1/2 = 6 bags.
Sample justification (any of the three strategies earns full marks)

I used the reciprocal because both numbers were mixed numbers. It works because dividing asks how many 1 1/4-pound portions fit inside 7 1/2 pounds, and multiplying by 4/5 counts those portions.

Diagnostics the page issues automatically

An answer of 9 (12 × 3/4) is caught as "a product, not a quotient".
An answer of 1/16 or 5/6 is caught as flipping the dividend instead of the divisor.
The Level 2 extension asks the student to make the quotient exceed the dividend — any divisor under 1 does it, and the explanation is what is being scored.

5
Part 2 · Error analysis

Repair the Depot Order

Multiplied by the divisor instead of by its reciprocal → 16 banners
The note wrote 12 ÷ 3/4 → 12 × 3/4 = 9. It changed the operation but left the fraction as it was.
Correct: 12 × 4/3 = 16.
The distractor "flipped the dividend" is the near-miss — accept it as a diagnosis only in discussion, not on the page.
Sample explanation

The note multiplied by 3/4, but dividing asks how many 3/4-metre pieces fit in 12 metres, so the answer has to be bigger than 12, not smaller. Flip the divisor, not the operation alone.

Partner compare

The comparison panel stays locked until the student's own bus count is correct, so a student cannot read their answer off a partner's. Two students with different Level 2 numbers will disagree legitimately — the conversation you want is about how each treated the remainder.

6
Part 3 · Design and defend — all five Pre-Unit lessons

Design the Depot Order

There is no single answer. Score the design against four rules.

1. riders ÷ seats leaves a non-zero remainder — the page rejects an even division.
2. pack price is not a whole dollar — the page rejects an integer price.
3. ribbon per banner is less than 1 — the page rejects 1 or more.
4. the written defence makes a claim, cites the student's own numbers, and answers an objection.

All three quotients are checked against the student's OWN inputs
buses = ceiling(riders ÷ seats) · packs = floor(budget ÷ price) · banners = floor(ribbon ÷ per-banner).
Any legal design passes, and no answer is ever printed — a wrong quotient is answered with the misconception ("you counted only the full buses"), not the value.
Sample defence at Level 2

I claim the hardest division to be sure about was the buses, because the arithmetic was the easiest and the answer was still wrong the first time. Someone might object that 30 is what the calculator says, but a calculator does not know that a student cannot ride 0.75 of a bus, so I ordered 31.

Reflection (Step 6). Three sentences, not an essay. The one worth reading closely is the last — "which Pre-Unit idea do you feel most ready to use in Grade 6?" — because Unit 3 opens on ratio reasoning that assumes exactly this division fluency.
