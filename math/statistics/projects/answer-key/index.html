Statistics Projects — Teacher Answer Key

Worked exemplars using each page's built-in example dataset, the expected relationship at every step, the misconceptions to watch for, and rubric anchors at each score point. For teachers only.

Statistics · Data & Distributions 6.SP.A.1 6.SP.B.4 6.SP.B.5 Teacher Use Only

These projects are open-ended, so this is not a single numeric key. Every student chooses their own dataset, so their numbers will differ. What is fixed — and what you actually grade — is the relationship at each step: the formula they must apply, whether their statistic is consistent with their own data, and whether their explanation says what the number means. Each phase below gives you (1) the exact numbers produced by the page's own "Use example numbers" button so you can verify the tool in front of a class, (2) the relationship a correct answer must satisfy for any dataset, (3) the common wrong moves, and (4) 4/3/2 anchor examples.

🔍

Version A — Class Data Detective

Context: surveying classmates about nightly sleep hours (or screen time), then finding center (mean, median), spread (range, MAD), and building a dot plot.

1
Measures of Center · 6.DS.4 / 6.SP.B.5

Step 1 — Input Dataset & Find Center

Students enter 6–10 survey values and press Calculate Center. The tool refuses to calculate with fewer than 6 values. Numbers below are the page's built-in example dataset.

Example dataset (8 values, hours)
8, 7, 9, 8, 10, 6, 8, 9
Relationships used
mean = sum ÷ count; median = middle of the ordered list
Mean ≈ 8.13 hours · Median = 8 hours
Mean: 8 + 7 + 9 + 8 + 10 + 6 + 8 + 9 = 65; 65 ÷ 8 = 8.125 ≈ 8.13 hours.
Median: order the data → 6, 7, 8, 8, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10 — careful, the ordered list is 6, 7, 8, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10. With an even count (8), average the two middle values: (8 + 8) ÷ 2 = 8.
Why they are close: this dataset has no extreme value, so the balance point (mean) sits almost exactly on the middle value (median).
Expected for ANY student dataset
Check these three things, not a specific number:
1. The mean lies between their smallest and largest value — if it does not, they summed or divided wrong.
2. They ordered the list before taking the median (this is the #1 error).
3. With an even count they averaged the two middle values; with an odd count they took the single middle value.
Common misconceptions
• Median from the unsorted list. Taking "the middle box on the screen" instead of the middle of the ordered data.
• Dividing by the wrong count. Dividing the sum by 10 because there are 10 input boxes, when only 8 were filled in.
• "The mean is the most common value." That is the mode; the mean is the balance point.
• Rounding too early. Rounding each value before summing changes the mean.
Sample Expert Response

"I surveyed 8 classmates about nightly sleep. The total was 65 hours, so the mean is 65 ÷ 8 = 8.13 hours. When I put the values in order (6, 7, 8, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10) there is an even number of values, so the median is the average of the two middle 8s, which is 8 hours. The mean and median are almost the same, which tells me nobody in my sample is an extreme outlier."

Rubric anchors — Measures of Center

4 (Expert): Both statistics correct for their own data and the student says what each one means in context ("8.13 hours is the balance point of my class's sleep").
3 (Proficient): Mean and median both computed correctly; explanation restates the procedure rather than the meaning.
2 (Developing): One statistic correct — typically the mean — with the median taken from an unsorted list, or a division by the wrong count.

2
Measures of Spread · 6.DS.4 / 6.SP.B.5c

Step 2 — Range and Mean Absolute Deviation

The tool prints a deviation table row-by-row, so students can be graded on the steps, not just the final MAD. Continuing with the example dataset (mean 8.125).

Range = 4 hours · MAD ≈ 0.91 hours
Range: 10 − 6 = 4 hours.
MAD: take |value − 8.125| for every value, add them, divide by 8.
Value 8 7 9 8 10 6 8 9 Sum
|value − 8.125| 0.125 1.125 0.875 0.125 1.875 2.125 0.125 0.875 7.25
MAD = 7.25 ÷ 8 = 0.90625 ≈ 0.91 hours — on average, each classmate is about 0.9 hours away from the class average.
Level 2 — Outlier Simulator (worked)
Add an outlier of 2 hours to the same dataset:
New mean = 67 ÷ 9 = 7.44 hours (down 0.69).
New median = middle of 2, 6, 7, 8, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10 = 8 hours — unchanged.
New range = 10 − 2 = 8 hours (doubled).
New MAD = 14.67 ÷ 9 ≈ 1.63 hours (nearly doubled).
The point of the simulator: one extreme value moves the mean, the range, and the MAD, but leaves the median where it was. That is exactly why the median is the better summary for skewed data — students must say this, not just record the numbers.
Common misconceptions
• Signed deviations. Forgetting the absolute value, so the deviations sum to 0 and the MAD comes out as 0.
• Deviations from the median. MAD is measured from the mean.
• "Range = the list of values." The range is one number: max − min.
• Dividing the deviation sum by the wrong count after adding the outlier (must be 9, not 8).
Rubric anchors — Measures of Spread

4 (Expert): Range and MAD correct with the full deviation table shown, and the student interprets MAD as a typical distance from the mean.
3 (Proficient): Range and MAD both correct; minor arithmetic slips in one or two deviation rows that do not change the conclusion.
2 (Developing): Range correct but MAD missing or computed without absolute values; steps not shown.

3
Dot Plot & World Comparison · 6.DS.5 / 6.SP.B.4

Step 3 — Interactive Dot Plot & Comparisons

Students stack dots with the + / − steppers under each axis value, then press Check My Plot. The check passes only when the frequencies match their Step 1 dataset exactly.

8 dots total: 6→1, 7→1, 8→3, 9→2, 10→1
For the example dataset the dot counts are 1 at 6, 1 at 7, 3 at 8, 2 at 9, and 1 at 10 — and the dots must total the same count used for the mean (8).
Shape: the tallest stack is at 8 with a short tail toward 6, so the distribution is roughly symmetric with a slight left tail. That is why mean ≈ median here.
World comparison — how to grade it
The step links the CDC sleep page and names the recommended 8–10 hours range for this age group. Accept any figure the student correctly cites from the linked source; grade the comparison sentence, which must (a) state their own class mean, (b) state the outside figure, and (c) say higher/lower and by how much. Example: "Our class mean is 8.13 hours, which is inside the recommended 8–10 hour range but only 0.13 hours above the low end."
Common misconceptions
• One dot per person per column. Placing a single dot at 8 instead of three when three classmates reported 8.
• Bars instead of stacks. Treating the dot plot like a bar graph of categories.
• Comparing a mean to a maximum. Comparing their class mean to the top of the recommended range and concluding the class is "way below."
• Skew named backwards. Students often call a left tail "skewed right."
Rubric anchors — Dot Plot Creation & Comparison

4 (Expert): Plot matches every frequency, and the student describes the distribution's shape and center and ties it to a cited outside statistic.
3 (Proficient): Plot matches the dataset; comparison to the national figure is present but thin.
2 (Developing): One or two frequencies off, or the comparison names a source without any numbers.

4
Classmate Comparison · 6.DS.1 / 6.SP.A.1

Step 4 — Compare Statistics With a Partner

Students type a partner's mean, median, range, and MAD; the tool builds a side-by-side table with the differences. Worked example uses our dataset versus a partner reporting mean 7.50, median 7.50, range 5, MAD 1.25.

My class is higher on average and more consistent
Mean: 8.13 − 7.50 = +0.63 hours (my class sleeps more on average).
Median: 8 − 7.50 = +0.50 hours.
Range: 4 − 5 = −1 hour (my data is packed into a narrower window).
MAD: 0.91 − 1.25 = −0.34 hours → the smaller MAD means my class's sleep values cluster more tightly around their average.
Common misconceptions
• "Bigger MAD = better." A larger MAD means more variability, not better sleep.
• Comparing spread with the mean. Concluding one group is more consistent because its mean is higher.
• Ignoring sample size. A partner who surveyed 6 people and one who surveyed 10 are not equally reliable — an Expert response says so.
Sample Expert Response

"My MAD is 0.91 hours and my partner's is 1.25 hours. Both classes average around 8 hours, but my partner's data is more spread out, so their classmates are less alike in how much they sleep. My mean is 0.63 hours higher than theirs, which is a small difference compared to how much individual students vary — so I would not claim my class 'sleeps more' as a fact from this sample."

Rubric anchors — Comparisons

4 (Expert): Compares center and spread to both the partner and the outside data, and states a limitation (sample size, who was surveyed).
3 (Proficient): Both comparisons completed with correct differences.
2 (Developing): Only the means compared, or differences stated without saying which is larger.

5
Summary Pitch · 6.DS.6 / 6.SP.B.5d

Step 5 — Finish & Submit

What the pitch must contain
3–5 sentences that use the student's own numbers: the statistical question, the mean and median, at least one spread statistic (range or MAD), what the dot plot's shape shows, and one honest limitation. A pitch with no numbers cannot score above a 2 regardless of how well written it is.
Sample 4-point pitch (example dataset)

"I asked 8 classmates: how many hours do you sleep on a school night? Their answers ranged from 6 to 10 hours, a range of 4 hours. The mean is 8.13 hours and the median is 8 hours; because those are so close, no single person is pulling the average. The MAD is 0.91 hours, so a typical classmate is within about an hour of the average. My dot plot piles up at 8 hours, which is right at the low end of the recommended 8–10 hours — and with only 8 people surveyed, I would want a bigger sample before saying this is true for the whole grade."

Rubric anchors — Summary Pitch

4 (Expert): Uses center, spread, and shape together, in context, with a stated limitation.
3 (Proficient): Reports mean, median, and one spread statistic correctly.
2 (Developing): General statements about sleep with one or no statistics, or numbers that do not match the student's own data.

Version B — Real-World Data Investigation

Context: logging daily high temperatures for a chosen city, then finding the five-number summary and IQR, choosing a display, and matching the climate to a region.

1
Center & Spread · 6.DS.4 / 6.SP.B.5

Step 1 — Measures of Center & Spread

Level 1 collects 5 temperatures, Level 2 opens 8 boxes. The tool refuses to calculate with fewer than 5 values. Numbers below are the page's built-in example (San Diego, 5 days).

Example dataset (°F)
72, 75, 78, 70, 75
Ordered
70, 72, 75, 75, 78
Mean 74.0 °F · Median 75 °F · Range 8 °F · IQR 5.5 °F
Mean: 70 + 72 + 75 + 75 + 78 = 370; 370 ÷ 5 = 74.0 °F.
Median: odd count (5) → the 3rd ordered value = 75 °F.
Range: 78 − 70 = 8 °F.
Q1: median of the values below the median (70, 72) = (70 + 72) ÷ 2 = 71 °F.
Q3: median of the values above the median (75, 78) = (75 + 78) ÷ 2 = 76.5 °F.
IQR: 76.5 − 71 = 5.5 °F.
Five-number summary: 70 · 71 · 75 · 76.5 · 78.
Expected for ANY student dataset
The page uses the standard middle-school quartile method: with an odd count, the median itself is excluded from both halves before finding Q1 and Q3. Q1 ≤ median ≤ Q3 always, and IQR ≤ range always — if a student's IQR exceeds their range, they mixed up a quartile.
Common misconceptions
• Including the median in both halves when the count is odd, which shifts Q1 and Q3.
• Quartiles from the unsorted list.
• "IQR = Q3." The IQR is a difference, not a value from the data.
• Mean 74 vs median 75. Students often assume they must match; the small gap here is caused by the single low 70.
Rubric anchors — Statistical Question & Calculations

4 (Expert): Question anticipates variability ("What was the daily high in San Diego over 5 days?" — not "What is today's high?"), and all five statistics are correct for their own data.
3 (Proficient): Statistical question posed; center and spread correct.
2 (Developing): Question is not statistical (a single-answer question), or one quartile is wrong so the IQR is off.

2
Visual Display · 6.DS.5 / 6.SP.B.4

Step 2 — Choose the Display

Correct choice: Box Plot
The box plot is the only one of the three options that draws the five-number summary directly: whiskers at 70 and 78, box edges at Q1 = 71 and Q3 = 76.5, median line at 75. The box width is the IQR (5.5 °F).
Why not the others: a dot plot shows every individual day but never marks quartiles; a histogram groups days into bins (70–79 °F) and hides the individual values and the quartiles.
The page's checker accepts only "Box Plot" and runs a no-giveaway hint ladder on wrong answers (nudge → structural hint → reveal), so a student who reaches the answer on the third try has seen it revealed, not reasoned it — check the written explanation.
Common misconceptions
• "The histogram shows spread best" — it shows frequency by bin, not quartiles.
• Reading the box as "half the temperatures are 5.5 degrees." The box spans the middle 50% of days, from 71 °F to 76.5 °F.
• Median = middle of the box. The median line is rarely centered; here it sits right of center because the upper values are tightly packed.
Rubric anchors — Visual Display

4 (Expert): Chooses the box plot and explains it by naming Q1, the median, Q3, and the IQR on their own generated plot.
3 (Proficient): Chooses the box plot with a correct but generic reason.
2 (Developing): Plot renders but the display choice or its justification is wrong.

3
Compare Regions · 6.DS.6b / 6.SP.B.5c

Step 3 — Contrast Your Spread Against Regional Data

IQR 5.5 °F → Coastal Region
The page's reference table gives coastal regions an IQR of about 5–10 °F and desert regions about 15–25 °F. Our example IQR of 5.5 °F sits at the low end of the coastal band, which matches the San Diego dataset.
Grading rule for any dataset: the match must follow from their IQR. A student with an IQR of 18 °F who selects "Coastal" is wrong even if their city is on a coast; a student whose IQR is 12 °F should select "Neither / Other" and say why.
Level 2 — Science inquiry (thermal absorption)
Expected reasoning: water has a very high specific heat capacity, so a large body of water absorbs and releases heat slowly. Sea breezes carry that moderated temperature inland, which compresses the daily high-to-low swing and shrinks the IQR. Deserts have dry air, little water, and sparse vegetation, so the ground heats fast during the day and radiates heat away quickly at night — a large daily swing and a large IQR. Accept any answer that connects water's slow heating/cooling to a smaller spread.
Common misconceptions
• Matching on the mean instead of the IQR. "Phoenix is hot and my mean is high, so I am a desert" ignores what the step asks — deserts are identified by variability, not by being hot.
• Confusing range with IQR when reading the table (the table's bands are IQRs).
• Forcing a match. "Neither / Other" is a legitimate 4-point answer when the IQR falls between the bands and the student explains that.
Rubric anchors — Regional Comparison

4 (Expert): Region matched from their own IQR with the numeric band quoted, plus a scientific reason for the variability.
3 (Proficient): Correct region for their IQR with a brief justification.
2 (Developing): Region selected with no numeric support, or chosen from the city's reputation rather than the data.

4
Partner Compare · 6.DS.6b / 6.SP.A.3

Step 4 — Swap Data Logs

Students enter a partner's median and IQR; the tool reports both differences. Worked example pairs our San Diego log with a partner logging Phoenix: median 88 °F, IQR 19 °F.

Median differs by 13.0 °F · IQR differs by 13.5 °F
Center: |75 − 88| = 13.0 °F — the partner's city is typically 13 degrees warmer.
Spread: |5.5 − 19| = 13.5 °F — the partner's daily highs swing more than three times as widely as ours.
Conclusion a 4 must reach: the two differences answer different questions. The median difference says how warm; the IQR difference says how predictable. Our climate is cooler and far more consistent; theirs is hotter and much more variable.
Common misconceptions
• Adding the two differences into a single "difference score."
• "Higher median = more variable." Center and spread are independent.
• Comparing logs of different lengths without noticing (5 days vs 8 days at Level 2).
Rubric anchors — Partner Comparison

4 (Expert): Both differences correct and interpreted separately as "how warm" versus "how predictable."
3 (Proficient): Both differences correct with one combined interpretation.
2 (Developing): Only one statistic compared, or the difference is reported with no meaning attached.

5
Weather Analysis Pitch · 6.DS.6 / 6.SP.B.5d

Step 5 — Finish & Submit

What the pitch must contain
3–5 sentences using the student's own mean, median, and IQR, naming the display they chose and the region they matched. The pitch is where the five-number summary becomes a claim about a real place.
Sample 4-point pitch (example dataset)

"I asked what the daily high temperature in San Diego was over a 5-day stretch. The highs were 70, 72, 75, 75, and 78 °F, giving a mean of 74.0 °F and a median of 75 °F. I chose a box plot because it shows the whole five-number summary at once: whiskers at 70 and 78, a box from 71 to 76.5, and the median line at 75. My IQR is only 5.5 °F, which lands in the coastal band of 5–10 °F — the ocean absorbs and releases heat slowly, so the middle half of my days sit within about five degrees of each other. Five days is a small sample, so I would log a full month before calling this the city's normal pattern."

Rubric anchors — Pitch

4 (Expert): Uses center, spread, display, and region together with a stated limitation.
3 (Proficient): Contains mean, median, and IQR correctly.
2 (Developing): Incomplete pitch or statistics that do not match the student's own data table.
