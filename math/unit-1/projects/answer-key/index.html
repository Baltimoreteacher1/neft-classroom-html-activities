Unit 1 Projects — Teacher Answer Key

Worked solutions for both versions of the Unit 1 culminating project (Number Sense). Covers Version A: Block Party Planner and Version B: Build-a-Bot Budget Lab.

For teachers — worked solutions using the built-in Level 1 example values. Both projects use the student's own real numbers, so grade the method, not one fixed answer. The GCF and LCM solutions below use the values that auto-fill when a student selects Level 1 (GCF and LCM only). The budget and cost-per-guest / cost-per-robot steps have no fixed defaults and no budget cap, so those show a clearly labeled sample calculation you can adapt to any student's numbers. Every number below was re-traced from the exact formula each live calculator uses.
Version A  ·  Design & Build

🎉 Block Party Planner

Standards: 6.NOS.2 • 6.NOS.3 • 6.NOS.4 • Steps: Goodie Bags (GCF) → Entertainment (LCM) → Budget & Cost per Guest → Compare → Finish

1 Step 2 — Build Identical Goodie Bags (GCF · 6.NOS.4)

Input Level 1 Example Worked Solution Final Answer
Number of stickers (g-stickers) 24
Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24
Factors of 36: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36
GCF(24, 36) = 12

Each bag: 24 ÷ 12 = 2 stickers
Each bag: 36 ÷ 12 = 3 granola bars
12 goodie bags
(2 stickers + 3 bars each)
Number of granola bars (g-bars) 36

The calculator checks the student's typed GCF against gcd(a, b) and only says "Correct!" on an exact match.

2 Step 3 — Sync the Entertainment Schedule (LCM · 6.NOS.4)

Input Level 1 Example Worked Solution Final Answer
Music every (min) (l-music) 5
Multiples of 5: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35…
Multiples of 7: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35…

First common multiple = 35
Verification: 35 ÷ 5 = 7 (7 songs) ✓
              35 ÷ 7 = 5 (5 bubble bursts) ✓
Both meet at minute 35
Bubbles every (min) (l-bubble) 7

Note: 5 and 7 are coprime, so the example LCM is their product, 35. Different student inputs (e.g. 6 and 8) give a different LCM (24).

3 Step 4 — Track the Budget & Set a Fair Price per Guest (Decimal Operations & Division · 6.NOS.3 · 6.NOS.2)

Three decimal fields (Food, Decorations, Activities) are added for a total; the total is then divided by the number of guests. There is no budget cap and no "remaining" figure in the app. Sample numbers below are illustrative only.

Field Sample Amount Running Total
Food & drinks (c-food) $85.00 $85.00
Decorations (c-deco) $35.00 $120.00
Activities (c-act) $30.00 $150.00
Total cost
85.00 + 35.00 + 30.00 = $150.00
Cost per guest (25 guests)
$150.00 ÷ 25 guests = $6.00 per guest
Check: 25 × $6.00 = $150.00 ✓

Formula the app uses: (food + deco + act) ÷ guests. Grade the place-value alignment of the decimal sum and the division check.

L2 Level 2 — Distributive Property Extension (6.NOS.4)

At Level 2 the GCF auto-fill is cleared (students use their own numbers) and a text box (distrib) asks them to rewrite their two supply totals as GCF × (a + b). The checker accepts the exact form g(a + b) where a = stickers ÷ GCF and b = bars ÷ GCF.

Sample Level 2 Answer (using the 24 / 36 example)

GCF(24, 36) = 12
24 + 36 = (12 × 2) + (12 × 3) = 12 × (2 + 3) = 12 × 5 = 60 total items
Accepted input format: 12(2 + 3) or 12*(2+3)

Sample Expert Written Pitch (Final Deliverable)

"In our block party plan, we will make 12 identical goodie bags because GCF(24, 36) = 12 — each bag holds 2 stickers and 3 granola bars with nothing left over. The music and bubble machine line up again every 35 minutes (LCM of 5 and 7), so that is when we schedule the biggest crowd moment. Our total cost is $150.00 — $85.00 food + $35.00 decorations + $30.00 activities. Splitting that among 36... (my real number of) guests gives a fair share; with 25 guests it is $6.00 per person, and we checked it: 25 × $6.00 = $150.00."

Sample 4 / 3 / 2 Rubric Scoring Notes

Expert (4) responses demonstrate: GCF and LCM both stated correctly with reasoning shown (listing factors or multiples); decimal budget total accurate to the penny with place value lined up; cost-per-guest calculation shown step by step and verified by multiplying back; written pitch names every calculated number with correct units and connects each number to its operation.

Proficient (3): All numerical answers correct but the written pitch may omit one number or reasoning step.

Developing (2): At least two steps attempted; minor decimal/place-value or factor errors; pitch present but incomplete.

3 Step 4 — Parts Budget & Cost per Robot (Decimal Operations & Division · 6.NOS.3 · 6.NOS.2)

Three decimal cost fields (Motors, Sensors, Frames) are added for a total parts cost; the total is divided by the number of robots. There is no budget cap and no "remaining" figure in the app. Sample numbers below are illustrative only.

Field Sample Amount Running Total
Motors (c-motors) $200.00 $200.00
Sensors (c-sensors) $100.00 $300.00
Frames (c-frames) $84.00 $384.00
Total parts cost
200.00 + 100.00 + 84.00 = $384.00
Cost per robot (8 robots)
$384.00 ÷ 8 robots = $48.00 per robot

L2 Level 2 — Distributive Property Extension (6.NOS.4)

Same structure as Version A: rewrite the two supply totals as GCF × (a + b) in the distrib box.

Sample Level 2 Answer (using the 16 / 24 example)

GCF(16, 24) = 8
16 + 24 = (8 × 2) + (8 × 3) = 8 × (2 + 3) = 8 × 5 = 40 total items
Accepted input format: 8(2 + 3) or 8*(2+3)

Sample Expert Written Memo (Final Deliverable)

"Coach, using GCF(16, 24) = 8 we can build 8 identical tool kits, each with 2 cables and 3 sensors and no leftovers. Our two robots finish charging at the same time every 24 hours (LCM of 6 and 8). Our total parts cost is $384.00 ($200.00 motors + $100.00 sensors + $84.00 frames); split across 8 robots that is $48.00 per robot, and I checked it: 8 × $48.00 = $384.00. I recommend approving this order."

Sample 4 / 3 / 2 Rubric Scoring Notes

Expert (4) responses demonstrate: GCF of the two part counts correctly identified with kit contents stated; LCM charging hour correct with reasoning; the decimal parts total accurate with place value maintained; cost-per-robot computed and verified with a multiplication check; memo gives a clear recommendation and cites every number computed.

Proficient (3): All numerical results correct; memo uses most numbers but may skip the multiplication check or a kit-content detail.

Both Versions  ·  Self → Peer → World Structure

💬 Guidance for the Compare (Peer) & World Steps

Both projects follow a Self → Peer → World arc using the student's own real numbers, so the Compare-step answers are student-specific. There is no world "meter," computed average, difference-card grid, or "What-If Lab" in the shipped apps — the World step is a static reference range table and the Compare step outputs a plain text readout.

S Self (student-specific)

Grade the method, not a fixed number. Each tool recomputes live (oninput). A correct response shows: GCF/LCM via factors or multiples, decimal sums lined up by place value, and division checked by multiplying back. Selecting Level 1 auto-fills the GCF/LCM example values used above (24 / 36 and 5 / 7 for A; 16 / 24 and 6 / 8 for B).

P Peer — Compare Step (text readout)

Students type a partner's results into two fields (Version A: partner bags p-bags and partner cost per guest p-per; Version B: partner kits p-kits and partner cost per robot p-per). The app prints a text readout stating who made more and whose cost is lower. There is no single right answer — check that the comparison and reflection cite real numbers.

Worked example (Version A): If my cost per guest is $6.00 and my partner's is $9.20, the readout says "Your party costs $3.20 less per guest" (6.00 − 9.20 = −3.20). Bags 12 vs 10 → "You made 2 more."
Worked example (Version B): Kits 8 vs partner 6 → "You built 2 more." Cost per robot $48.00 vs partner $55.00 → "Your team costs $7.00 less per robot."

W World — Reference Range Table (static)

The World step shows a built-in reference table of real-world price ranges and asks the student to write one sentence about where their own value fits. There is nothing to compute here; grade the written comparison.

Version A — Cost-per-guest ranges (as shown in the app):

So the example student's $6.00 per guest is below even the small-town range — a strong sentence would note that and explain why (many guests sharing a modest total).
