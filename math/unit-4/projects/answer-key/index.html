Version A — Pop-Up Shop Owner

Context: Running a pop-up shop using discount pricing (10% / 20% / 25% off), unit-rate bundle deals, and sales tax across states.

1
Percent Discount · 6.AT.4

Step 2 — Set Your Prices (Discount Options)

Students enter three of their own product prices; the calculator shows the amount saved / amount paid at 10%, 20%, and 25% off. Numbers below use an example item priced at $20.00.

Example item price
$20.00
Formulas used
save = price × %; pay = price − save
$18.00 · $16.00 · $15.00 (pay prices)
10% off: $20.00 × 0.10 = $2.00 saved → pay $18.00
20% off: $20.00 × 0.20 = $4.00 saved → pay $16.00
25% off: $20.00 × 0.25 = $5.00 saved → pay $15.00
Sample Expert Response

"My candle is priced at $20.00. A 10% discount saves $2.00, so the shopper pays $18.00. A 20% discount saves $4.00 (pay $16.00), and a 25% discount saves $5.00 (pay $15.00). Each percent is a part of the price: I multiply the price by the decimal form of the percent to find the savings, then subtract."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Percent Discounts

4 (Expert): All three save/pay pairs correct for the student's own price; shows the two-step process (price × percent to find savings, then subtract).
3 (Proficient): Pay prices correct for all three discounts.
2 (Developing): Some values correct; common error is subtracting the percent number itself (e.g. 20 − 10) instead of taking a percent of the price.

2
Unit Rates · 6.AT.2 · 6.AT.2

Step 3 — Unit Rate Deals (Deal A vs Deal B)

Students enter two bundle deals (quantity + total price), compute each unit rate (price ÷ quantity), and pick the lower rate as the better value. Example numbers below.

Deal A (example)
3 items for $7.50
Deal B (example)
2 items for $5.20
Deal A wins ($2.50 vs $2.60 per item)
Deal A: $7.50 ÷ 3 = $2.50 per item
Deal B: $5.20 ÷ 2 = $2.60 per item
$2.50 < $2.60 → Deal A is the better value (lower unit rate). The self-check accepts each rate within ±$0.01 and requires the lower-rate deal to be chosen.
Sample Expert Response

"Deal A is 3 items for $7.50, so the unit rate is $7.50 ÷ 3 = $2.50 per item. Deal B is 2 items for $5.20, so its unit rate is $5.20 ÷ 2 = $2.60 per item. Because $2.50 is less than $2.60, Deal A is the better value — the buyer pays less per item even though the total price is higher."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Unit Rates (6.AT.2 / 3b)

4 (Expert): Both unit rates computed correctly (price ÷ quantity); lower-rate deal chosen with an explicit comparison; explains that the better deal is the lower cost per item, not the lower total.
3 (Proficient): Both unit rates correct; better deal identified.
2 (Developing): One rate correct or a division error; may pick the deal with the lower total price instead of the lower unit rate.

3
Sales Tax · 6.AT.4

Step 4 — Add Sales Tax

Students enter a subtotal and pick a location. The tool always shows a standard 6% line plus the selected state's rate. Tax = subtotal × rate; total = subtotal + tax. Example subtotal $40.00.

Example subtotal
$40.00
Default location
Maryland (6%)
$42.40 total at 6%
Standard 6%: $40.00 × 0.06 = $2.40 → total $42.40 (same as the Maryland default).
Built-in state rates and totals on $40.00:
Oregon 0% & Delaware 0% → $0.00 tax, total $40.00
Hawaii 4% → $1.60 tax, total $41.60
Maryland 6% → $2.40 tax, total $42.40
California 7.25% → $2.90 tax, total $42.90
Tennessee 9.55% → $3.82 tax, total $43.82
Sample Expert Response

"My subtotal is $40.00. At the standard 6% rate, tax is $40.00 × 0.06 = $2.40, so the total is $42.40. If I shop in Tennessee (9.55%) the tax jumps to $40.00 × 0.0955 = $3.82 for a $43.82 total, but in Oregon (0%) I pay just $40.00 — sales tax depends on the state's rate."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Sales Tax

4 (Expert): Tax amount and total correct for the student's subtotal at 6% and at least one state rate; explains that tax is a percent of the subtotal and varies by state.
3 (Proficient): Standard 6% total correct.
2 (Developing): One error, e.g. adding the rate as dollars, or computing tax on the wrong amount.

Level 2 Challenges · 6.AT.4 · 6.AT.3c

Level 2 — Break-Even Markup, Supplier, Stacked Promo

Break-Even Markup: retail $15.00, markup 25%
Wholesale cost is fixed at $12.00; you want a 20% discount to land the sale price exactly on $12.00.
Retail = cost ÷ (1 − 0.20) = $12.00 ÷ 0.80 = $15.00
Check: 20% off $15.00 = $3.00 off → $12.00 (break-even).
Markup % = (15 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = 25%. The self-check accepts retail ≈ $15.00 and markup ≈ 25%.
Supplier (lb → oz): Option B at $0.27/oz
Option A: $3.60 ÷ 12 oz = $0.30 per oz
Option B: 1.5 lb = 1.5 × 16 = 24 oz; $6.48 ÷ 24 oz = $0.27 per oz
$0.27 < $0.30 → Option B is the better deal, $0.03 cheaper per ounce. (Converting pounds to ounces is the 6.AT.3c measurement-conversion step.)
Stacked Discount + Tax: total $42.40
Selling price $50.00 with a 20% weekend discount → $50.00 × 0.80 = $40.00 sale price.
Tax at 6%: $40.00 × 0.06 = $2.40.
Final total: $40.00 + $2.40 = $42.40. Retail systems tax the discounted subtotal.
Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Level 2

4 (Expert): All three challenges correct with work shown — cost ÷ 0.80 for break-even, lb → oz conversion before dividing, and tax on the discounted subtotal.
3 (Proficient): Two of three correct, or all answers correct with limited explanation.
2 (Developing): One challenge correct; common errors are dividing by 0.20 instead of 0.80, forgetting to convert pounds to ounces, or taxing the pre-discount price.

Part 2 (Peer) & Part 3 (World) · Guidance

Peer Swap, World Tax Data & Level 2

Part 2 — Peer (who is the smarter shop?)
Students swap shops and type the partner's main item price and the partner's bundle deal (quantity + price). The tool computes the partner's deal unit rate, then renders me / partner / difference cards comparing both the main item price and the bundle unit rate, labeling the lower one as the better value.

Look for: a correct unit-rate computation for the partner's deal (price ÷ quantity) and a comparison that names the lower price / lower per-item rate — not simply the lower total.
Part 3 — World (real state sales-tax rates)
The Step 4 location dropdown offers these real state rates:
Oregon 0% · Delaware 0% · Hawaii 4% · Maryland 6% (default) · California 7.25% · Tennessee 9.55%.

Worked example: a $40.00 subtotal in Tennessee (9.55%): tax = $40 × 0.0955 = $3.82 → total = $43.82. In Oregon (0%) the total stays $40.00; in California (7.25%) it is $42.90.

Ethics reflection ("is cheapest always best?"): accept answers noting that a very low price can attract customers but may cut into profit or signal lower quality; "cheapest" is not automatically "best."
Sample Level 2 Response — supplier conversion & stacked promo

"For my supplier choice, Option B is 1.5 lb = 24 oz at $6.48, so $6.48 ÷ 24 = $0.27/oz, cheaper than Option A's $3.60 ÷ 12 = $0.30/oz — I convert pounds to ounces before comparing. For the stacked promo, my $50 selling price drops 20% to $40, then 6% tax adds $2.40, for a $42.40 checkout total."

Rubric Guidance — Comparison (Peer & World)

4 (Expert): compares own final price to both a partner and a real state rate; explains the difference with correct percent reasoning; addresses the "cheapest is not always best" prompt thoughtfully.
3 (Proficient): completes peer and world comparisons with mostly-correct reasoning.
2 (Developing): one comparison attempted; reasoning thin or numbers missing.

Version B — Smart Shopper Showdown

Context: Smart shopping using unit prices (better buy), percent savings on a sale, and a grocery bill with sales tax.

1
Unit Price · 6.AT.2 · 6.AT.2

Find the Better Buy

Package A price (default)
$3.60
Package A units (default)
12
Package B price (default)
$5.25
Package B units (default)
20
Package B wins (lower unit price)
Package A: $3.60 ÷ 12 units = $0.3000 per unit
Package B: $5.25 ÷ 20 units = $0.2625 per unit
$0.2625 < $0.3000 → Package B is the better buy.
Even though Package B costs more upfront ($5.25 vs $3.60), each unit is cheaper by $0.0375.
Sample Expert Response

"Package A costs $3.60 for 12 units, giving a unit price of $0.30 per unit ($3.60 ÷ 12). Package B costs $5.25 for 20 units, giving a unit price of $0.2625 per unit ($5.25 ÷ 20). Even though Package B has a higher sticker price, its unit price is lower by about $0.04, making it the better buy for a budget-conscious shopper."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Unit Price (6.AT.2/3b)

4 (Expert): Both unit prices computed correctly ($0.30 and $0.2625); Package B identified as better buy with explicit comparison ("$0.2625 < $0.30"); explains the apparent paradox that the bigger, more expensive package is cheaper per unit.
3 (Proficient): Both unit prices correct; better buy correctly identified.
2 (Developing): One unit price correct or a minor division error; may choose Package A because its total cost is lower (misunderstanding unit price).

Percent Discount · 6.AT.4

Which Store's Deal Is Cheaper?

Store 1 original price
$80.00
Store 1 discount
25%
Store 2 original price
$75.00
Store 2 discount
15%
Store 1 is cheaper ($60.00 vs $63.75)
Store 1: $80.00 × (1 − 0.25) = $80.00 × 0.75 = $60.00
  Alternative: Discount = $80 × 0.25 = $20 off → $80 − $20 = $60.00

Store 2: $75.00 × (1 − 0.15) = $75.00 × 0.85 = $63.75
  Alternative: Discount = $75 × 0.15 = $11.25 off → $75 − $11.25 = $63.75

Store 1 is cheaper. You save $3.75 by choosing Store 1 even though it started at a higher price.
Sample Expert Response

"Store 1 sells the item for $80.00 with a 25% discount: $80 × 0.75 = $60.00 after the sale. Store 2 sells for $75.00 with a 15% discount: $75 × 0.85 = $63.75 after the sale. Store 1 is the better deal at $60.00 — $3.75 cheaper than Store 2, despite having a higher original price."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Percent Discounts (6.AT.4)

4 (Expert): Both final prices correct ($60.00, $63.75); Store 1 identified as cheaper with the savings amount ($3.75); explains the counterintuitive result (higher original price, deeper discount → lower final price).
3 (Proficient): Both prices correct; cheaper store identified.
2 (Developing): One price correct or minor percent-computation error (e.g., computed 15% of $80 instead of $75).

Tip Percent & Measurement Conversion · 6.AT.4 · 6.AT.3c

Tip the Server & Convert Units

Part A — Tip Calculation

Restaurant bill (default)
$20.00
Tip % (default)
15%
$3.00 tip / $23.00 total
Tip amount: $20.00 × (15 ÷ 100) = $20.00 × 0.15 = $3.00
Total bill with tip: $20.00 + $3.00 = $23.00

Part B — Measurement Conversion (default: 5 feet → inches)

Value to convert (default)
5
From → To (defaults)
feet → inches
60 inches
Conversion factor: 12 inches / 1 foot
5 feet × (12 inches / 1 foot) = 60 inches
The "feet" units cancel, leaving inches. That's rate reasoning!

Other default dropdowns: if a student selects a different pair, they can use this method: value × factor from the CONV table in the source JS.
Sample Expert Response

"After shopping I had lunch and left a 15% tip on a $20.00 bill: $20 × 0.15 = $3.00 tip, for a $23.00 total. Using rate reasoning, I also converted 5 feet to inches by multiplying by the rate (12 inches per foot): 5 × 12 = 60 inches. The foot units canceled, confirming the conversion."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Tip & Conversion (6.AT.4/3d)

4 (Expert): Tip ($3.00) and total ($23.00) both correct; conversion shown as a unit-fraction multiplication (5 × 12 in/ft = 60 in) with units explicitly canceling; connects method to "rate reasoning."
3 (Proficient): Tip and conversion both numerically correct.
2 (Developing): Tip correct but conversion set up without the unit-fraction framework; or conversion answer correct but tip computation contains arithmetic error.

Decision & Quick Check · 6.AT.2 · 6.AT.2

Shopping Verdict + Quick Check

Quick Check — 4 granola bars for $12.00; unit price per bar?
What is the unit price (price per bar)?
$3.00 per bar
$12.00 ÷ 4 bars = $3.00 per bar. The JS accepts exactly the number 3.
Sample Expert Response — Shopping Verdict (Full Deliverable)

"Based on my unit price research, Package B is the better buy at $0.2625 per unit vs. Package A's $0.30 per unit — a savings of $0.0375 per unit. For the item I compared between stores, Store 1 is cheaper at $60.00 after a 25% discount, beating Store 2's $63.75 after a 15% discount. At the restaurant I tipped 15%, which came to $3.00, for a total of $23.00; and I converted 5 feet to 60 inches using rate reasoning (5 × 12 in/ft). My overall shopping strategy: always compute the unit price before choosing a package, and compare post-discount prices — not original sticker prices — when comparing stores."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Communication

4 (Expert): Verdict cites all four phase results (Package B unit price $0.2625, Store 1 final price $60.00, tip $3.00 and total $23.00, conversion 60 inches); gives a general shopping strategy drawn from the math; reasoning is clear and uses correct mathematical vocabulary (unit price, discount, rate reasoning).
3 (Proficient): Uses most numbers; package and store choices correctly stated.
2 (Developing): Verdict is vague or missing phase results (e.g., no unit prices mentioned, or store chosen without showing the post-discount prices).

Part 2 (Peer) & Part 3 (World) · Guidance

Peer Swap, National Unit-Price Data & Level 2

Part 2 — Peer (whose pick is the better buy?)
Students swap a real product and type the partner's price and number of units. The tool computes the partner's unit price, renders me / partner / difference cards, and labels the lower unit price as the better buy. Students compare against their own better-buy unit price from Part 1.

Look for: a correct unit-price computation for the partner (price ÷ units) and a comparison that names the lower-per-unit option, not the lower total price.
Part 3 — World (national grocery unit prices & tip norms)
Built-in average U.S. grocery unit prices used by the tool (approximate):
Milk $4.10/gal · Eggs $3.20/dozen · Bread $2.90/loaf · Bananas $0.62/lb · Chicken breast $4.30/lb · Rice $1.05/lb · Bottled water $0.55/16oz · Granola bar $0.65/bar · Apples $1.55/lb · Cheese $5.40/lb.

Tip norms shown: 15% (okay), 18% (good), 20% (great).

Worked example: a $0.2625/bar granola unit price vs. the $0.65/bar average → the student's buy is cheaper than the national average. Tip check: $20 × 0.18 = $3.60 tip → $23.60 total.

Ethics reflection ("is cheapest always best?"): accept answers noting quality, waste of buying more than needed, or perishability — cheapest per unit is not automatically the best buy.
Sample Level 2 Response — promo stacking & percent decrease / tip percent

"Package B costs $5.25 for 20 units ($0.2625/unit). On a 15% sale it costs $5.25 × 0.85 = $4.4625, so the new unit price is $4.4625 ÷ 20 = $0.2231/unit — a 15% decrease, and now even further below Package A's $0.30/unit. For tips: a $20 bill with an 18% tip is $3.60 tip and a $23.60 total; the tip is $3.60 ÷ $23.60 × 100 ≈ 15.3% of the total (less than 18% because the total includes the bill plus the tip)."

Rubric Guidance — Comparison (Peer & World)

4 (Expert): compares own unit price to both a partner and a national average; explains differences with correct rate reasoning; addresses the "cheapest is not always best" prompt thoughtfully.
3 (Proficient): completes peer and world comparisons with mostly-correct reasoning.
2 (Developing): one comparison attempted; reasoning thin or numbers missing.
