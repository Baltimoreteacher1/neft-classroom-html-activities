Math Universe

What the gameplay shows

In a Math Universe game the mathematics is the control. A reactor's output is the student's expression, and the lift moves by exactly that much. A wrong expression is a lift at the wrong height that the student can see, rewind and repair, not a red X.

Six kinds of evidence, kept separate

  • Completion: the student reached the end of a level. On its own this says little.
  • Accuracy: each mathematical decision and whether the world accepted it, with the exact expression or setting used. A wrong decision is tagged with the site's misconception names (for example, exponent as multiplication when base × exponent would have matched).
  • Reasoning: predictions made before a change and whether they held; demonstrations shown to Ori; short explanations the student wrote. Explanations are saved for you to read and are never auto-scored.
  • Independence: accepted decisions made with no support open.
  • Supports: which scaffolds were used (the running product, a sentence starter, the Time Machine, guided travel).
  • Transfer: Ori, the apprentice, attempts a task it was never shown. Ori only gets it right for the ideas the student has demonstrated by building a model or a counterexample. Telling Ori the answer is recorded and never counts.

What is never measured

Speed. There is no clock in any game, nothing is scored on reaction time, and no duration is recorded. The game waits while a student thinks. "Guide me there" moves the character to the next machine for students for whom movement is a barrier; the mathematics is the same.

Where you see it

When a level is finished the result goes to the same place as the other site games (EduPulse scores and the misconception digest), and to Canvas when the game is launched from a Canvas assignment. The student sees a summary of their own decisions, revisions, predictions and teaching at the end of each level. Progress is saved on the student's device; no name or personal information is collected by the game.

Revisions are the point

The Time Machine lists every mathematical decision, marks the one that changed the result, and lets the student go back to just before it, try a different idea, and compare the two paths. A student who revises a wrong expression shows more than one who guessed right; the "ideas you fixed" count records that.
