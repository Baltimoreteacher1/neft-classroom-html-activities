Equations & Inequalities · Grade 6 · Standards 6.AT.8, 6.AT.8, 6.AT.8, 6.AT.9

For teachers only — do not distribute to students. Worked solutions below use each project's default input values. Students who change the inputs will get different but mathematically similar answers; the same reasoning steps apply. Use the "Sample Expert Response" paragraphs as 4-point anchor papers.
Version A

Escape Room Architect

Standards: 6.AT.8 (substitution check) · 6.AT.8 (writing equations with variables) · 6.AT.8 (solving one-step equations)

How the deployed Version A works. Students (1) name their room and pick a theme, (2) write one secret-code equation (addition or subtraction) from a code and constant they choose, (3) choose and solve one of four built-in combination locks from a dropdown (multiplication / division), (4) check the lock by substitution and rate its difficulty against real escape-room stats, (5) swap one clue with a partner and solve theirs, then (6) write a pitch and generate a report. Live calculators verify each step by substitution as students type. The worked solutions below use natural example inputs; the four dropdown locks are fixed, so their solutions are always the same.

Step 1 — Set the Code (secret-code equation) 6.AT.8 · 6.AT.8 · Add/Subtract

The student picks a secret code (the solution x), an operation, and a constant. The app builds the clue equation x ± constant = result and checks it by substitution. Numbers vary by student — verify with the live "Verify Clue Equation" button. Two worked examples:

Addition example — secret code 12, constant 5:

x + 5 = 17
Result = code + constant = 12 + 5 = 17, so the clue is x + 5 = 17.
To crack it, a player uses the inverse (subtract 5): x = 17 − 5 = 12 (the secret code).
Substitution check: 12 + 5 = 17 ✓ Verified
Secret code x = 12

Subtraction example — secret code 12, constant 5:

x − 5 = 7
Result = code − constant = 12 − 5 = 7, so the clue is x − 5 = 7.
Inverse (add 5): x = 7 + 5 = 12.
Substitution check: 12 − 5 = 7 ✓ Verified
Guard: for subtraction the app requires code ≥ constant, so players never solve into negative numbers.
Secret code x = 12

Step 2 — Solve a Clue Equation (four built-in locks) 6.AT.8 · Multiply / Divide

Students pick one lock from a fixed dropdown and solve it with the inverse operation. These four equations and their solutions never change. (Level 1 hints point to the whole-number locks; Level 2 hints point to the larger/decimal locks — but all four are always available.)

The Cipher Lock:

3x = 36
x is multiplied by 3 → inverse is division: divide both sides by 3.
x = 36 ÷ 3 = 12
Substitution check: 3 × 12 = 36 ✓ Verified
The Cipher Lock: x = 12

The Laser Grid Lock:

x / 4 = 8
x is divided by 4 → inverse is multiplication: multiply both sides by 4.
x = 8 × 4 = 32
Substitution check: 32 ÷ 4 = 8 ✓ Verified
The Laser Grid Lock: x = 32

The Chamber Lock:

12x = 180
x is multiplied by 12 → divide both sides by 12.
x = 180 ÷ 12 = 15
Substitution check: 12 × 15 = 180 ✓ Verified
The Chamber Lock: x = 15

The Trapdoor Lock:

x / 2.5 = 12
x is divided by 2.5 → multiply both sides by 2.5.
x = 12 × 2.5 = 30
Substitution check: 30 ÷ 2.5 = 12 ✓ Verified
The Trapdoor Lock: x = 30

Step 3 — Check & Compare 6.AT.8 · Substitution

Substitution check: the app rebuilds the chosen lock as a fill-in statement and accepts only the exact matching values:
  3x = 36 → 3 × 12 = 36
  x / 4 = 8 → 32 ÷ 4 = 8
  12x = 180 → 12 × 15 = 180
  x / 2.5 = 12 → 30 ÷ 2.5 = 12

Real-world stats table (static reference): average solve rate 30%–40%, average time to solve 45–60 minutes, cost per person $25–$35. The difficulty rating dropdown and the reflection are open response — any reasoned comparison of the student's lock to these figures is acceptable.

Step 4 — Partner Clues (swap & solve) 6.AT.8 · 6.AT.8 · Verify

The student enters a partner's one-step equation, names the operation and the inverse, applies the number to both sides, solves, and enters a substitution check (LHS = RHS). The app parses addition, subtraction, multiplication (e.g. 5x = 45) and division (e.g. x / 3 = 10) forms and confirms every field. Worked example — partner wrote 5x = 45:
  Operation: Multiplication → Inverse: Division by 5
  x = 45 ÷ 5 = 9
  Substitution: 5 × 9 = 45, so LHS 45 = RHS 45 ✓ Verified
Look-for: the correct inverse operation and a balanced substitution, not just a guessed number.

Complete Answer Key Summary — The Four Built-in Locks

The Cipher Lock: 3x = 36  →  x = 12   (verify: 3 × 12 = 36 ✓)
The Laser Grid Lock: x / 4 = 8  →  x = 32   (verify: 32 ÷ 4 = 8 ✓)
The Chamber Lock: 12x = 180  →  x = 15   (verify: 12 × 15 = 180 ✓)
The Trapdoor Lock: x / 2.5 = 12  →  x = 30   (verify: 30 ÷ 2.5 = 12 ✓)

The four dropdown locks are fixed, so these solutions are always correct. The secret-code equation in Step 1 and the partner clue in Step 4 use student-chosen numbers; use the live calculators to check any custom values quickly.

Sample Expert Response (4/4 Deliverable)

Sample Escape Room Blueprint:
"My escape room is called The Vault of Numbers (theme: Secret Spy Lab). My secret code is 12, so my clue equation is x + 5 = 17 — players subtract 5 from both sides to get x = 12, and 12 + 5 = 17 checks out. For my combination lock I chose The Chamber Lock, 12x = 180. Since x is multiplied by 12, I divided both sides by 12 to get x = 15, and 12 × 15 = 180 verifies it. I rated it Hard, since a solve rate near 30% matches a tougher puzzle. My partner's clue was 5x = 45; I divided both sides by 5 to get x = 9 and checked 5 × 9 = 45. Every lock was solved with an inverse operation and verified by substitution."
Sample 4 / 3 / 2 Rubric Scoring Guide (matches the in-app rubric):
4 — Expert: Secret-code equation (6.AT.8) written correctly with a variable and matching the theme; combination lock (6.AT.8) solved step-by-step by naming the correct inverse operation; substitution check (6.AT.8) shown; partner clue solved and a real-world comparison made; pitch uses the student's real numbers.
3 — Proficient: Equations and lock solved correctly; at least one substitution check shown; pitch present with real numbers.
2 — Developing: Equation or lock attempted with a computation error, or the substitution check / pitch is missing or incomplete.
Version B

Fundraiser Goal Tracker

Standards: 6.AT.8 (substitution) · 6.AT.8 (writing equations & inequalities with variables) · 6.AT.8 (one-step equations) · 6.AT.9 (writing and graphing inequalities)

How the deployed Version B works. Students plan a class fundraiser and work through five steps: (1) set a goal and amount raised and solve an addition equation for the amount still needed, (2) write and graph a milestone inequality in y on a number line, (3) write and solve a multiplication equation for the number of items to sell, (4) compare progress with a classmate, and (5) write a pitch and generate a report. The worked solutions below use the app's default input values (goal $500, raised $180, price $5, milestone target $350); students who change them get different but similar answers.

Step 1 — Fundraiser Goal & Progress 6.AT.8 · Addition Equation

Default inputs:

Fundraiser goal = $500
Amount already raised = $180
180 + x = 500
Equation model: raised + x = goal
  180 + x = 500
Inverse operation: Subtraction undoes addition. Subtract 180 from both sides:
  x = 500 − 180 = $320

Substitution check:
  $180 + $320 = $500 ✓ Verified

Interpretation: The class still needs to raise $320 to meet the $500 goal. (The app accepts many equivalent forms, e.g. x + 180 = 500, 500 − 180 = x.)
x = $320 more needed

Step 2 — Progress Milestone (Inequality) 6.AT.9 · Writing & Graphing Inequalities

Default inputs:

Milestone = "Stretch Goal"
Target amount = $350
Symbol = > (more than) · variable y
y > 350
Inequality written: y > 350 (the app uses the variable y = total amount raised).
Read as: "The total raised (y) must be more than $350."

Solution set: all values greater than 350 — infinitely many solutions (351, 400, 500, …); 350 itself is not included because the symbol is strictly >.

Number-line description: draw an open circle at 350 (open = 350 excluded, because > is strict) and shade the ray extending to the right. If a student instead chooses ≥, use a closed circle at 350; the < and ≤ options shade to the left.

Test-value check (from the app's logic):
  y = 350: 350 > 350 → FALSE (not in the set)
  y = 351: 351 > 350 → TRUE
  y = 400: 400 > 350 → TRUE
  y = 300: 300 > 350 → FALSE
Inequality: y > 350  |  Open circle at 350, shade right
Chromebook note: the checker accepts typed >= for ≥ and <= for ≤, so students who pick those symbols can still type their inequality.

Step 3 — Plan Product Sales 6.AT.8 · Multiplication Equation

Default inputs:

Price per item = $5 (Popcorn)
Amount still needed = $320
5n = 320
Equation model: price × n = amount needed  (n = items to sell). The "needed" amount comes straight from Step 1: goal − raised = 500 − 180 = 320.
  5n = 320
Inverse operation: Division undoes multiplication. Divide both sides by 5:
  n = 320 ÷ 5 = 64 items

Divisibility check: 320 ÷ 5 = 64 exactly (no remainder), so no rounding is needed here.
Substitution check: 64 × $5 = $320 ✓ Verified

Interpretation: selling 64 items of popcorn at $5 raises the remaining $320. If a student instead chooses a price that does not divide evenly, the app rounds the item count up (ceiling) so the plan reaches at least the amount.
n = 64 items to sell
Level 2 (custom price) examples, same $320 needed:
  Cookie Dough at $8: 8n = 320 → n = 320 ÷ 8 = 40 items.
  Candy Bars at $2: 2n = 320 → n = 320 ÷ 2 = 160 items.
Real-world benchmark table (static reference): bake sale / car wash $200–$800, product sales $2,000–$5,000, fun run / walk-a-thon $3,000–$8,000. The benchmark reflection is open response.

Step 4 — Compare With a Classmate 6.AT.8 · Compare Percents

Students enter a classmate's goal and amount raised; the app computes each person's percent to goal, the dollar and goal differences, and names who is closer. Using the student's defaults (goal $500, raised $180) versus an example partner (goal $400, raised $250):
  Me: 180 ÷ 500 = 36% to goal.
  Partner: 250 ÷ 400 = 62.5% ≈ 63% to goal.
  Difference: 62.5% − 36% = 26.5% ≈ 27 percentage points; the partner is closer to their goal.
  The partner's goal ($400) is $100 lower than the student's ($500); the student has raised $70 less ($180 vs $250).
Look-for: reasoning uses percent of goal, not just raw dollars (a bigger goal needs more dollars).

Sample Expert Response (4/4 Deliverable)

Sample Fundraiser Plan using the default numbers:
"Our fundraiser is for classroom library books. Our goal is $500 and we have already raised $180, so 180 + x = 500 gives x = $320 more needed (verify: 180 + 320 = 500). Our stretch-goal milestone is y > 350 — an open circle at 350 on the number line, shaded to the right, because $350 itself is not past the milestone. We will sell popcorn at $5 each: 5n = 320, so n = 64 items (verify 5 × 64 = 320). A classmate is 63% to their $400 goal while we are 36% to our $500 goal, so they are about 27 percentage points closer even though our goal is larger. Please support our library-book fundraiser!"
Sample 4 / 3 / 2 Rubric Scoring Guide (matches the in-app rubric):
4 — Expert: Addition equation (6.AT.8) set up, solved with step-by-step subtraction, and verified; milestone inequality (6.AT.8, 6.AT.9) graphed accurately with correct open/closed circle and shading, and a test value evaluated; multiplication equation (6.AT.8) solved with division and any fractional items rounded up; percent progress compared with a classmate and against real-world benchmarks.
3 — Proficient: All equations and the inequality solved correctly and graphed properly; at least one substitution check shown; both classmate and benchmark comparisons attempted.
2 — Developing: Equations set up correctly but with a calculation error, or the number-line graph / test evaluation is wrong; only one of the two comparisons completed.
