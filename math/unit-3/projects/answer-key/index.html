Unit 3 · Ratios & Rates 6.AT.1 6.AT.2 6.AT.3 Teacher Use Only

For teachers — worked solutions using the default values. Students now compute every value themselves: each step hides its result until the student enters their own answer and it checks out, so a blank readout on a projected screen means that step is not solved yet, not that the page is broken. Students who change inputs get different but similarly-structured answers. Each phase shows: default inputs used, step-by-step arithmetic, the correct final answer, and the exact quick-check answer. Sample expert responses and rubric guidance are included for scoring.

Version A — Smoothie Bar Designer

Context: Opening a school smoothie bar using ratios, equivalent ratios, unit rates, and better-buy comparisons.

1
Writing & Simplifying Ratios · 6.AT.1

Write Your Smoothie Recipe Ratio

Strawberries — cups (default)
3
Bananas — cups (default)
2
3:2
Three forms of the ratio:
• Colon form: 3:2
• Word form: 3 to 2
• Fraction form: 3/2

Simplification: GCF(3, 2) = 1, so the ratio is already in simplest form. For every 3 cups of strawberries use 2 cups of bananas.
Sample Expert Response (deliverable starter)

"My smoothie recipe uses 3 cups of strawberries to 2 cups of bananas. I can write this ratio as 3:2, 3 to 2, or 3/2. Because GCF(3, 2) = 1, the ratio is already in simplest form, meaning for every 3 cups of strawberries I need exactly 2 cups of bananas to keep the same taste."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Writing & Simplifying Ratios

4 (Expert): Writes all three forms correctly (3:2, 3 to 2, 3/2), recognizes GCF = 1 (already simplest form), and explains what it means in context ("for every 3 cups of strawberries…").
3 (Proficient): All three forms correct and recognizes the ratio is already simplified.
2 (Developing): Ratio written but one form is missing, or wrongly "simplifies" 3:2 to something else.

Equivalent Ratios & Ratio Tables · 6.AT.3

Scale Up Your Recipe

Base cups of strawberries (1×)
3
Base cups of bananas (1×)
2
4 Equivalent Ratios
The Scale-Up table asks students to fill the ×2, ×5, ×10, and ×20 columns. Multiply both parts of the base ratio (3:2) by the number of smoothies:
Smoothies Strawberries (cups) Bananas (cups) Ratio
1 (base) 3 2 3:2
2 (×2) 6 4 6:4 → 3:2
5 (×5) 15 10 15:10 → 3:2
10 (×10) 30 20 30:20 → 3:2
20 (×20) 60 40 60:40 → 3:2
Every row is an equivalent ratio because each simplifies to 3:2. The recipe taste is identical at every scale. The app marks a cell green only when it equals base × multiplier.
Sample Expert Response

"To serve more smoothie customers I scaled my base recipe (3 cups strawberries, 2 cups bananas). For 5 smoothies I need 15 cups and 10 cups; for 20 smoothies I need 60 cups and 40 cups. All rows simplify to 3:2, confirming the taste stays the same no matter how many I make."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Equivalent Ratios / Ratio Table

4 (Expert): All four scaled columns correct (6:4, 15:10, 30:20, 60:40); explains why each ratio is equivalent ("multiplied both parts by the same factor"); connects back to the recipe context.
3 (Proficient): All table cells numerically correct.
2 (Developing): Some cells correct; pattern not explained.

Unit Rate & Better Buy · 6.AT.2 · 6.AT.3

Price Your Smoothies Fairly

Part A — Price per Smoothie (Unit Rate)

Fruit/ingredient cost (default)
$12.00
Liquids cost (default)
$5.00
Supplies cost (default)
$3.00
Smoothies per batch (default)
5
$4.00 per smoothie
Total batch cost = $12.00 + $5.00 + $3.00 = $20.00
Unit rate = Total cost ÷ Number of smoothies
$20.00 ÷ 5 = $4.00 per smoothie
Set the selling price above $4.00 to make a profit.

Part B — Better Buy (bulk fruit, priced per pound)

Deal A pounds
5 lbs
Deal A cost
$10.00
Deal B pounds
3 lbs
Deal B cost
$7.00
Deal A is the better buy
Deal A: $10.00 ÷ 5 lbs = $2.00 per pound
Deal B: $7.00 ÷ 3 lbs = $2.33 per pound (rounded from 2.333…)
Deal A is cheaper per pound, saving about $0.33 per pound. The lower unit price wins even though Deal A costs more in total.
Sample Expert Response

"Each smoothie costs $4.00 to make ($20.00 ÷ 5), so I must sell each for more than $4.00 to profit. For bulk fruit, Deal A ($10.00 for 5 lbs = $2.00/lb) beats Deal B ($7.00 for 3 lbs = $2.33/lb) by about $0.33 per pound, so I will order Deal A even though its total price is higher."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Unit Rate & Better Buy

4 (Expert): Cost per smoothie $4.00 correct with the $20.00 sum and division shown; both price-per-pound rates computed ($2.00, $2.33), Deal A identified as better buy with a contextual explanation ("saves ~$0.33/lb").
3 (Proficient): Unit rate correct; better buy correctly identified.
2 (Developing): Unit rate attempted; better buy missing or incorrect (e.g., chose Deal B because its total price is lower).

Comparing Ratios & Rates · 6.AT.1 · 6.AT.3

Compare Recipes With a Partner

Step 4 compares the student's recipe and cost to a classmate's. Partner values are entered live during the interview, so there are no fixed defaults; the worked example below uses the student's own defaults (3:2, $4.00/smoothie) versus a sample partner (2:2 recipe, $4.50/smoothie).

My recipe (Strawberries : Bananas)
3 : 2
My cost per smoothie
$4.00
Partner recipe (example)
2 : 2
Partner cost per smoothie (example)
$4.50
Mine is fruitier and $0.50 cheaper per smoothie
Recipe blend (strawberries per banana):
Me: 3 ÷ 2 = 1.50 : 1  |  Partner: 2 ÷ 2 = 1.00 : 1
1.50 > 1.00, so my smoothie is stronger/fruitier in strawberries.

Cost per smoothie:
Mine $4.00 vs partner $4.50 → mine is cheaper by $0.50 per smoothie.
The app reduces each recipe to an "x : 1" ratio and compares cost-per-smoothie directly, so any partner numbers work.
Sample Expert Response — Pitch & Reflection (Full Deliverable)

"My smoothie recipe uses a ratio of 3 cups of strawberries to 2 cups of bananas (3:2, already simplest form). To serve more customers I can scale up — 5 smoothies need 15 cups strawberries and 10 cups bananas — keeping the same 3:2 ratio. Each smoothie costs $4.00 to make ($20.00 ÷ 5), so I priced mine at $5.50 for a $1.50 profit. I chose Deal A bulk fruit because it costs $2.00 per pound vs Deal B's $2.33 — saving about $0.33 per pound. Compared to my partner, my blend is fruitier (1.50:1 vs 1.00:1) and $0.50 cheaper per smoothie."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Comparison & Communication

4 (Expert): Reduces both recipes to an "x : 1" ratio, correctly states who is fruitier, compares cost per smoothie, and the pitch cites the ratio, scaled table, unit rate, and better buy.
3 (Proficient): Both comparisons correct and a clear decision is given.
2 (Developing): One comparison correct, or the pitch is vague/missing key numbers.

Version B — Sports Stats Scout

Context: Junior sports analyst computing unit rates, equivalent ratios, and season projections for two players.

1
Scoring Unit Rate · 6.AT.2

Compute the Athlete's Points-Per-Minute Rate

Step 1 scouts a single athlete. Inputs have no fixed defaults; the Level 1 scaffold prefills the example below (Steph Curry, 60 points in 30 minutes).

Athlete name (Level 1 example)
Steph Curry
Points scored (Level 1 example)
60
Minutes played (Level 1 example)
30
2.00 points per minute
Unit rate = Points ÷ Minutes
60 points ÷ 30 minutes = 2.00 points per minute
This is the ratio 2.00 : 1 — for every 1 minute of play the athlete scores 2 points.
Sample Expert Response

"The athlete scored 60 points in 30 minutes, giving a scoring unit rate of 2.00 points per minute (60 ÷ 30). Written as a ratio that is 2:1 — every minute on the floor produces about 2 points, which I can use to project longer games and full seasons."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Unit Rate

4 (Expert): Unit rate correct (2.00 ppm); division shown (60 ÷ 30); interprets it as a rate/ratio ("2 points every 1 minute").
3 (Proficient): Unit rate numerically correct.
2 (Developing): Division set up incorrectly (e.g., minutes ÷ points instead of points ÷ minutes).

Ratio Table & Rates · 6.AT.3

Project Season Performance

Scoring rate (from Step 1)
2.00 pts/min
Avg. minutes per game (Level 1 example)
30
60.0 points per game
First find points per game: Points per minute × minutes per game = 2.00 × 30 = 60.0 PPG. Then multiply by the number of games (the app projects 10, 20, 40, and 80 games):
Games Played Projected Points Calculation
1 game 60.0 pts Unit rate (2.00 × 30)
10 games 600 pts 60.0 × 10
20 games 1200 pts 60.0 × 20
40 games 2400 pts 60.0 × 40
80 games 4800 pts 60.0 × 80
Every row is an equivalent ratio of 60.0 : 1 game (the per-game rate never changes). Note: these are clean scaffold numbers. Real scouting data (e.g., 0.8 pts/min × 30 min = 24 PPG) gives realistic season totals with the same method.
Sample Expert Response

"My athlete scores 2.00 points per minute and plays about 30 minutes per game, so their per-game average is 60.0 points (2.00 × 30). My ratio table projects 600 points in 10 games, 1200 in 20, 2400 in 40, and 4800 in 80 games. Every row keeps the same 60.0 : 1 rate, so they are all equivalent ratios."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Ratio Table / Projection

4 (Expert): Finds PPG (2.00 × 30 = 60.0), all four projection cells correct (600, 1200, 2400, 4800); explains the pattern ("multiply the per-game rate by games played") and that each row is an equivalent ratio.
3 (Proficient): All projection cells numerically correct.
2 (Developing): Two or more cells correct; pattern partially shown.

Comparing Averages (Rates) · 6.AT.2 · 6.AT.3

Compare Two Players' Averages

Step 3 compares two players by points per game (higher is better). The Level 1 scaffold prefills Steph Curry (60 pts in 2 games) and Aja Wilson (50 pts in 2 games).

Steph Curry — points
60
Steph Curry — games
2
Aja Wilson — points
50
Aja Wilson — games
2
Steph 30.00 PPG  |  Aja 25.00 PPG — Steph by 5.00
Average = points ÷ games:
Steph Curry: 60 ÷ 2 = 30.00 PPG
Aja Wilson: 50 ÷ 2 = 25.00 PPG
30.00 > 25.00, so Steph has the better average, a difference of 5.00 PPG. (The app accepts each PPG within ±0.05 and then asks the student to pick the higher rate.)
Sample Expert Response

"To compare the two players fairly I found each average: Steph Curry is 60 ÷ 2 = 30.00 PPG and Aja Wilson is 50 ÷ 2 = 25.00 PPG. Steph has the higher average by 5.00 points per game, so on scoring rate alone Steph is the better scorer."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Comparing Averages

4 (Expert): Both averages correct (30.00, 25.00) with division shown; picks the higher PPG (Steph) and states the 5.00 PPG difference.
3 (Proficient): Both averages correct and the higher one chosen.
2 (Developing): One average correct, or chose the lower PPG as "better."

Partner Compare & Communication · 6.AT.3

Partner Compare & Scouting Report

Step 4 scales the student's athlete and a partner's athlete to a common number of games (rate × games). The Level 1 scaffold uses the Step-3 Player 1 (Steph, 60 pts / 2 games = 30.0 PPG) versus a partner athlete (LeBron, 80 pts / 4 games = 20.0 PPG) over 10 common games.

My athlete PPG (Steph, 60 ÷ 2)
30.0
Partner athlete PPG (LeBron, 80 ÷ 4)
20.0
Common base (games)
10
In 10 games: Mine = 300 pts  |  Partner = 200 pts
Scale each athlete's PPG to 10 games:
Mine: 30.0 PPG × 10 games = 300.0 pts
Partner: 20.0 PPG × 10 games = 200.0 pts
My athlete scores more over the common base, leading by 100.0 points in 10 games.
Sample Expert Response — Scouting Report (Full Deliverable)

"Coach, my scouted athlete scores 2.00 points per minute (60 ÷ 30). At about 30 minutes a game that projects to 60.0 points per game and 600 points over 10 games. Comparing scorers, Steph Curry averages 30.0 PPG (60 ÷ 2) versus Aja Wilson's 25.0 PPG (50 ÷ 2), so Steph is the stronger scorer by 5.0 PPG. Against my partner's athlete (LeBron, 20.0 PPG), mine leads 300 to 200 over 10 games. I recommend starting my athlete based on the higher, consistent scoring rate."

Sample 4/3/2 Rubric Guidance — Partner Compare & Communication

4 (Expert): Scales both athletes to the common base correctly (300, 200), states who leads and by how much, and the scouting report cites the unit rate, the season projection, and the player comparison with a clear recommendation.
3 (Proficient): Both scaled correctly and a clear recommendation is given.
2 (Developing): One scaled correctly, or the report is vague/missing key numbers.

How Does Your Player Compare?

Sample Level 2 Response — Rate-as-data (preview of statistics)

"The player rates have a mean of about 9.1 points per game. The 'phenom' at 22.0 ppg is an outlier — far above everyone else. Without it the mean drops to about 7.2 ppg, which shows one outlier can pull the average up and make the typical player look stronger than they are."

Rubric Guidance — Level 2 (rate-as-data) & Careful Thinking

4: Computes the mean correctly, identifies the outlier (phenom), and explains how it pulls the mean up; careful-thinking note gives a real reason rates differ (league level, minutes, position, sport). 3: Mean and outlier correct. 2: Mean attempted or outlier named without explanation.
