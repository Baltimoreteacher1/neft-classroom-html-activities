Civic participation and arguing a local issue

Students role-play a city council hearing about an empty lot, voice stakeholder perspectives, evaluate each other's arguments and recommend a decision in writing.

Make this a game Edit the activity

Standards

C3
D2.Civ.10.6-8 [VERIFY]: Explain the relevance of personal interests and perspectives, civic virtues, and democratic principles when people address issues and problems in government and civil society.
WIDA ELD Standard
ELD-SS: Language for Social Studies
Key Language Use
Argue

Language demands

Discourse

  • Argue for a recommendation on a local issue while showing that you understand how different stakeholders see it.
  • Evaluate another speaker's argument by judging whether the reasons are relevant, supported and fair to other groups.
  • Connect a position to a real way of taking part in a community decision, such as public comment, a letter to the council or a petition.

Sentence

  • Conditional sentences that predict consequences: If the council builds a parking lot, then ___. If neighbors start a garden, ___.
  • Modal verbs for recommendations: The council should ___. Neighbors could ___. The city ought to ___.
  • Phrases that attribute a perspective: From the perspective of ___, ___. Many shop owners believe that ___.

Word / phrase

  • Civic action verbs: advocate, petition, testify, vote, volunteer, deliberate.
  • Evaluative adjectives for judging arguments: convincing, well-supported, one-sided, fair, vague.
  • The false friend compromise / compromiso: in English, compromise means a solution where each side gives up something.

Objectives

Content
Students will explain how community members with different interests take part in a local decision and evaluate arguments about whether an empty lot should become a community garden or a parking lot.
Language
Students will argue for a recommendation orally and in writing using modal verbs (should, could), a conditional sentence (If ___, then ___) and a phrase that attributes another stakeholder's perspective.

Sentence frames by proficiency band

Levels 1–2

  • I want a ___. (garden / parking lot)
  • It helps ___. (families / shops / students / older neighbors)
  • The city should ___.
  • I agree / disagree with ___.

Levels 3–4

  • The city council should ___ because ___.
  • If the lot becomes a ___, then ___.
  • From the perspective of ___, ___.
  • ___'s argument was convincing because ___.

Levels 5–6

  • Although ___ have a valid concern about ___, the council should ___ because ___.
  • A fair compromise might be ___, which would address both ___ and ___.
  • ___'s argument was one-sided because it did not consider ___.
  • Community members who want this outcome could ___, since that would let officials hear ___.

Activity: Community Lot Hearing

25 minutes · small groups

  1. Set the scene: 'An empty lot sits next to the bus stop on Maple Street. The city council will decide whether it becomes a community garden or a parking lot, and tonight it is holding a public hearing.' Explain that many ways to take part, such as speaking during public comment, writing to council members or volunteering, are often open to community members whether or not they can vote.
  2. Write four roles on the board: a grandmother who walks to the bus stop and wants space to grow vegetables with her grandchildren; a bakery owner whose customers cannot find parking; a student who wants a safe place to volunteer after school; a city planner who reports that parking spaces would bring in money for street repairs.
  3. Each student in a group of four takes one role and has three minutes to prepare a 30-second public comment using the frames for their band. Each comment must include a should sentence and an If ___, then ___ sentence.
  4. Run the hearing: one student acts as council chair and calls each speaker. Listeners score each comment on a quick checklist: relevant reason, specific detail, considers another group (one check each).
  5. Groups deliberate for three minutes and agree on a recommendation, which may be a compromise (for example, a garden with a few short-term parking spaces). One member reports the decision and the most convincing argument they heard.
  6. Each student writes a four-sentence recommendation to the council that names one way a community member could share it (letter, public comment or petition) and attributes at least one other stakeholder's perspective.

Five-minute warm-up

Four Corners: post Strongly agree, Agree, Disagree and Strongly disagree in the corners. Read a school-level statement, such as 'Students should help decide how the school spends money on new recess equipment.' Students move to a corner, talk with the people there for one minute and share one reason using: 'I ___ because ___.' Ask one student to restate a reason from a different corner: 'People in that corner think ___.'
