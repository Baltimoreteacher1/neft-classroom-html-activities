Rivers and early cities

Students write informational texts about how rivers supported early civilizations, organizing facts into categories and using precise social studies vocabulary.

Grades 6–8WritingELD-SSInform25 min

  1. Task 1 of 2 · Levels 3–4 · Inform · 60–130 words

    Many early cities grew up near rivers, such as the Nile, the Tigris and Euphrates, the Indus, and the Yellow River. Write an informational paragraph that describes three ways rivers helped people in these early cities.

    Plan

    Sentence starters

    0 words (goal: 60–130)

    Check yourself
    Sample answers

    Level 2 sample (23 words)

    River have water. People drink. Farm need water for food. Boat on river. People trade. Fish in river. River help city a lot.

    • Names three ways rivers helped (water, boats, fish).
    • Short sentences and phrases; some verb endings are missing.
    • Uses everyday words more than social studies words.

    Level 4 sample (65 words)

    Rivers helped early cities in many ways. One way is that rivers give water for drinking and for farming. Farmers dug canals to bring river water to their crops. In addition, rivers were used for transportation. People traveled by boat and traded goods with other cities. Another resource was food, because people catch fish in the river. Overall, rivers were very important for early cities.

    • Topic sentence, three supporting details and a conclusion.
    • Uses transitions such as One way, In addition and Overall.
    • Shifts between past and present tense (give, catch).
    • Uses some content words: canals, transportation, traded.

    Level 6 sample (96 words)

    Rivers supplied early cities with the resources that made settled life possible. First, rivers provided fresh water for drinking and for irrigation, as farmers dug canals to carry water to fields of wheat, barley and rice. Each year, some rivers flooded and left behind rich soil called silt, which made the land fertile. Rivers also served as highways; merchants transported grain, pottery and cloth by boat, which encouraged trade between distant cities. Finally, rivers offered fish, reeds and clay for building homes and making tools. Without these rivers, early cities could not have grown so large.

    • Clear topic sentence, organized details and a conclusion that sums up the main idea.
    • Varied sentences with which clauses, a semicolon and introductory phrases.
    • Precise social studies words: irrigation, silt, fertile, merchants.
    • Groups facts into categories (water, transportation, materials).

  2. Task 2 of 2 · Level 5 · Inform · 120–250 words

    Write a short informational report about one early river civilization of your choice. Include an introduction, at least two body paragraphs about how the river shaped daily life (for example, farming, trade or government), and a conclusion. Use facts you have learned in class.

    Plan

    Sentence starters

    0 words (goal: 120–250)

    Check yourself
    Sample answers

    Level 2 sample (26 words)

    Egypt is by Nile River. River flood every year. Farmer grow wheat. They make bread. Boat go on river. People trade. Nile very important for Egypt.

    • Gives several true facts about one civilization.
    • Short, simple sentences in a list; no paragraphs yet.
    • Some verb endings are missing (River flood, Farmer grow).

    Level 4 sample (118 words)

    Ancient Egypt grew along the Nile River in North Africa. The Nile was very important for the people who lived there.

    First, the river helped farming. Every year the Nile flooded and when the water go down it left black mud. The mud was good for growing wheat and barley. Farmers also make canals to water their fields when it was dry.

    In addition, the Nile was important for trade and travel. People used boats to carry stone, grain and other goods to different cities. Boats could go north with the current and south with the wind, so travel was easy.

    In conclusion, the Nile gave Egypt food and helped people trade, so Egypt became a powerful civilization.

    • Has an introduction, two body paragraphs and a conclusion.
    • Transitions (First, In addition, In conclusion) link the paragraphs.
    • Some tense errors (the water go down, farmers also make).
    • Includes accurate facts and some content words (current, canals, civilization).

    Level 6 sample (192 words)

    For thousands of years, ancient Egyptian civilization depended on the Nile River, which flows north through the desert to the Mediterranean Sea. Almost everything about daily life, from the food people ate to the way the government collected taxes, was shaped by this river.

    The Nile's yearly flood was the heart of Egyptian farming. Each summer, the river rose and spread over its banks. When the water went down, it left a layer of dark, fertile silt, so Egyptians called their land Kemet, or the black land. Farmers planted wheat, barley and flax in this soil and dug canals to irrigate their fields during the dry months.

    The Nile was also Egypt's main highway. Because the current flows north and the winds usually blow south, boats could travel in both directions with little effort. Traders shipped grain, papyrus and building stone along the river, connecting villages and cities. Officials even measured the height of the flood to predict the harvest and decide how much tax farmers should pay.

    In conclusion, the Nile provided food, transportation and a rhythm for the year. Without it, one of history's longest-lasting civilizations could not have existed.

    • The introduction states the focus, each body paragraph has one main idea, and the conclusion sums up.
    • Complex sentences with which, when and because show how facts are related.
    • Precise academic words: fertile, irrigate, current, officials, predict.
    • Specific, accurate details (Kemet, flax, papyrus) inform the reader.
