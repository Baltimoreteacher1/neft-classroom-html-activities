Why some ice melts faster

Students explain investigation results and design choices using cause-and-effect language about heat transfer, conductors and insulators.

Grades 6–8WritingELD-SCExplain25 min

  1. Task 1 of 2 · Levels 3–4 · Explain · 60–130 words

    In an experiment, two identical ice cubes were placed on two blocks at room temperature: one block made of metal and one made of plastic. The ice on the metal block melted much faster. Explain why.

    Plan

    Sentence starters

    0 words (goal: 60–130)

    Check yourself
    Sample answers

    Level 2 sample (18 words)

    Ice on metal melt fast. Metal give heat. Plastic no give heat fast. Metal is conductor. Plastic slow.

    • States the result and one cause.
    • Short sentences with missing verb endings.
    • Uses one science word (conductor).

    Level 4 sample (64 words)

    The ice on the metal block melted faster because metal is a good conductor of heat. Heat moves from the warm block into the cold ice. Metal let heat move very quickly, so the ice gets energy fast and melts. In contrast, plastic is an insulator. Heat moves slowly through plastic, so the ice melted slower. This shows that the material makes a difference.

    • Gives the result, the cause, a comparison and a conclusion.
    • Uses because, so and In contrast to show cause and effect.
    • Some verb errors (Metal let) and tense shifts.
    • Uses science words: conductor, insulator, energy.

    Level 6 sample (101 words)

    The ice on the metal block melted faster because metal is an excellent thermal conductor. Even though both blocks were at room temperature, which is warmer than ice, the metal transferred heat into the ice much more quickly. Its particles pass energy to neighboring particles rapidly, so a steady flow of heat reached the bottom of the cube. Plastic, on the other hand, is an insulator; it transfers heat slowly, so the ice on it absorbed energy at a lower rate. Surprisingly, metal often feels colder to the touch for the same reason: it pulls heat away from our hands quickly.

    • Gives a clear answer, explains the process step by step, then compares the two materials.
    • Complex sentences with Even though, which and so; a semicolon joins related ideas.
    • Precise words: thermal conductor, transferred, absorbed, at a lower rate.
    • Connects the result to an everyday experience to deepen the explanation.

  2. Task 2 of 2 · Level 5 · Explain · 120–250 words

    Engineers want to design a lunch box that keeps food cold for as long as possible. Explain how heat moves into a cold lunch, and explain how two design choices (for example, materials or features) would slow down that heat transfer. Use cause-and-effect language.

    Plan

    Sentence starters

    0 words (goal: 120–250)

    Check yourself
    Sample answers

    Level 2 sample (25 words)

    Heat go in box. Hot day heat go fast. Box need foam. Foam stop heat. Ice pack make cold. Food stay cold. Close lid good.

    • Names two design ideas (foam and an ice pack).
    • Very short sentences with missing verb endings.
    • Uses simple words; foam is a topic-specific word.

    Level 4 sample (125 words)

    Heat always moves from warm things to cold things. In a lunch box, heat from the warm air outside moves into the cold food, so the food slowly gets warm. Engineers can slow this down in two ways. One design choice is to put foam inside the walls of the box. Foam is a insulator, which slows heat transfer because it has a lot of tiny air pockets. Another choice is to add an ice pack. The ice pack absorb heat that gets inside, so the food stays cold longer. If the lunch box was made of thin metal, the food would get warm much faster because metal is a conductor. Consequently, foam walls and an ice pack keep the lunch cold for more time.

    • Explains the main idea, two design choices and a conclusion.
    • Uses because, so, which and If to link causes and effects.
    • Some errors (a insulator, the ice pack absorb).
    • Uses science words: insulator, conductor, heat transfer, absorb.

    Level 6 sample (192 words)

    Heat always flows from warmer objects to cooler ones until they reach the same temperature. On a warm day, the air and sunlight outside a lunch box carry heat toward the cold food inside, so without a good design, a sandwich or a carton of milk would warm up within an hour or two.

    One effective design choice is to build the walls with a layer of foam. Foam is filled with tiny trapped air pockets, and because air is a poor conductor, heat has a hard time passing through the walls. A second choice is to include a reusable ice pack. Instead of warming the food, any heat that leaks in is absorbed by the ice pack as it slowly melts, which keeps the temperature inside low for several hours. A tight-sealing lid also helps by preventing warm air from flowing in through gaps.

    If engineers used a thin metal box with no insulation, heat would be conducted inward quickly, and the food would spoil sooner. Consequently, combining insulated walls with an ice pack is the most effective way to slow heat transfer and keep lunch cold and safe to eat.

    • Opens with the science principle, explains each design choice, then contrasts it with a poor design.
    • Varied complex sentences (because..., Instead of..., If... would...) express cause and effect.
    • Precise words: poor conductor, absorbed, insulation, conducted inward.
    • A what-if comparison shows why the design choices matter.
