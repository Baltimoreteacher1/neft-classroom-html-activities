Colors and heat from the sun

Students read about a class experiment with colored jars and practice using data, explaining cause and effect with absorb and reflect, and evaluating why a test is fair.

Grades 4–5ReadingELD-SCExplain16 min

The Color Test

On a sunny afternoon, Kofi noticed that his black backpack felt hot, but his friend's white backpack felt cool. He wondered if color changes how much heat an object takes in from the sun. His class decided to test the question.

The students filled three jars with the same amount of cold water. They wrapped one jar in black paper, one in white paper and one in silver foil. They put a thermometer in each jar and placed all three jars in the same sunny spot on the playground.

Every 15 minutes, the students checked the thermometers and the temperature. At the start, the water in every jar was 15 degrees . After one hour, the water in the black jar was 27 degrees, the white jar was 20 degrees, and the silver jar was 18 degrees.

Their teacher, Ms. Lindqvist, explained the results. Sunlight carries . Dark colors most of the light that hits them, and that energy turns into heat. Light colors and shiny reflect much of the light away, so they warm up more slowly.

The class used what they learned. They suggested painting the roof of the school shed white to keep the tools inside cooler in summer. Kofi decided to wear lighter colors on hot days, too, and he now keeps his black backpack in the shade.

Word help
recorded
wrote down information to keep it (anotaron: escribieron información para guardarla)
energy
the power that makes things move, light up or get warm (energía: la fuerza que hace que las cosas se muevan, den luz o se calienten)
absorb
to take in and keep (absorber: tomar algo y quedárselo)
Celsius
a scale for measuring temperature (Celsius: una escala para medir la temperatura)
surfaces
the outside or top layers of things (superficies: las partes de afuera o de arriba de las cosas)
