Design Studio

Real design challenges where the mathematics decides whether the plan works. Constraints are numeric, choices are consequential, and two students who choose differently have to compute differently.

Design Studio is a curated layer over the projects the curriculum already runs. It adds one consistent flow and one place for the work to land — not a second project system.

The common flow

Eleven steps, every project

Architect challenges, unit culminating projects, and the year-end capstone all run this same sequence. A student who has done one knows how to start the next, and a teacher can drop into any project and know where the class is.

  1. Choose a challenge Pick the design problem you want to solve.
  2. Read the brief What is being built, and for whom.
  3. Review requirements The success criteria and the numeric constraints.
  4. Plan Sketch the approach before computing anything.
  5. Build or model Construct it — on paper, on a grid, or in Math Workbench.
  6. Calculate The mathematics the constraint actually requires.
  7. Test Check the design against every constraint.
  8. Revise Change the design and recompute. Record what changed and why.
  9. Defend Justify the solution with mathematical evidence.
  10. Save to My Portfolio With unit, standards, revisions, and date attached.
  11. Present or export Presentation mode, print, or export.

What makes a choice meaningful

A Design Studio choice has to change the arithmetic. Dimensions, materials, budgets, quantities, locations, target customers, environmental constraints, and design priorities all qualify. Picking a different colour or a different label does not — if two students' numbers come out identical, the choice was decoration.

Where the work lands

Portfolio and presentation

Design Studio uses the portfolio the curriculum already had. Each entry carries its project, unit, lesson, standards, project type, date, revision count, language support, evidence type, and completion status.

Presentation mode

When a student presents, editing controls disappear and the entry shows the problem, the process, the revisions, the mathematics, the final product, and the reflection. The revisions stay visible on purpose: the changed plan is the most interesting part of the work.

Being precise

Honest limits

  • Portfolio entries live in the student's browser. Clearing site data removes them unless the student exported first.
  • Peer feedback is captured in the page and is not routed between devices.
  • Some 3D modeling widgets need WebGL. Each has a 2D fallback, but the 3D view will be unavailable on older hardware.
  • Any student work shown in a public demonstration is synthetic. Real student artifacts would require consent and are listed as outstanding manual work.
