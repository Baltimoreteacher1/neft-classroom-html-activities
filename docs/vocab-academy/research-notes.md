# Vocabulary Academy — Research Notes

Status: working notes for the build, dated 2026-11-20. Author: literacy research role. Companion documents:
`word-selection.md` (how words are chosen) and `content-qa.md` (how content is checked).

## How to read this document

- **Scope.** Part A summarizes publicly documented features and instructional principles of established vocabulary
  programs, so that Vocabulary Academy can be designed with eyes open. It describes principles only. No proprietary word
  list, passage, exercise wording, sequence or layout is reproduced, and none may be used in the program (SPEC §9).
- **Evidence labels.** Part B separates (a) empirical research from (b) vendor claims. Every citation was either located
  during this research pass (marked **[located]**) or is a standard reference the author is certain exists but did not
  re-open in this pass (marked **[known]**). Anything with a detail the author could not confirm is marked **verify**.
  Effect sizes quoted below come from abstracts or secondary summaries, not from re-reading full papers, and are marked
  **verify** where the source was secondary. Check the primary paper before quoting any figure externally.
- **Method note.** Vendor descriptions come from the vendors' own sites and white papers. They are promotional and
  describe design intent, not demonstrated outcomes. Retail and blog descriptions of products were treated as weaker
  than the publisher's own pages, and where sources disagreed the disagreement is noted.

### A.6 Morphology-centered approaches

Sources **[located]** unless noted. These are practitioner and research-informed approaches, not single products.

- **Structured Word Inquiry (SWI; Real Spelling; Peter Bowers, Kirby & Bowers).** Students investigate how words are built
  from base elements, affixes and spelling conventions, forming and testing hypotheses about meaning and spelling. The
  theory behind it holds that morphology binds the sound, spelling and meaning of a word. Most descriptions come from
  advocates and training providers; the sources say the SWI-specific research base is promising but young.
- **Words Their Way, Derivational Relations stage (Bear, Invernizzi, Templeton & Johnston).** Word study through sorts and
  games focused on how related words are derived from a base or root, Greek and Latin roots, and spelling changes in
  derived words, aimed at upper elementary through adults. Cognate study is recommended for English learners. Grade
  placement varies across summaries; treat the placement as developmental rather than tied to a grade.
- **Bringing Words to Life: Robust Vocabulary Instruction (Beck, McKeown & Kucan; 2002, 2nd ed. 2013) [known].** Not a
  morphology program, but the most-cited practitioner framework for rich instruction. Principles (described, not quoted):
  choose words students are likely to meet again and can use in many contexts; give friendly student-level explanations
  instead of dictionary text; offer varied contexts; make students respond actively (compare, choose, explain, produce);
  provide many encounters over time; build a classroom culture of word consciousness.

## Part B. The evidence base, honestly separated

### B.1 Empirical research

**Robust vocabulary instruction.** Beck, McKeown & Kucan's _Bringing Words to Life_ **[known]** is a synthesis of
classroom design experiments and prior research, not a meta-analysis. Its core claims (rich, varied, repeated,
active encounters; student-friendly explanations) are consistent with the meta-analytic findings below. The earlier
classroom studies behind it (McKeown, Beck, Omanson & Pople 1985, on number of encounters and richness; **verify**
exact citation) used researcher-made measures of taught words.

**Tier 1 / 2 / 3 is a heuristic, not an empirical taxonomy.** The three-tier idea comes from the same authors **[known]**.
They present it as a rough guide for choosing words. It was not derived from a validated measurement model; categories
overlap, and a word's tier shifts with the learner and the text. We use it as a labelled design aid (`tier` in the
schema), not as a finding. Critics (for example Hiebert's work on word zones; **verify**) argue frequency and
morphological family size matter and that Tier 2 lists are inconsistent across publishers. See `word-selection.md`.

**Repeated meaningful exposures.** Stahl & Fairbanks (1986), _Review of Educational Research_ 56(1), 72–110
**[located]**: the strongest instruction paired definitional with contextual information, involved deeper processing, and
gave more than one or two exposures. Reported mean effect on researcher-made comprehension of passages containing taught
words was large (about 0.97) and on global comprehension measures small (about 0.30) (from secondary summaries; **verify**).
Elleman et al. later argued that this earlier analysis mixed effect sizes from designs with and without control
groups and did not weight by sample size, so its numbers should be treated cautiously.

**Explicit instruction effect sizes.**

- Elleman, Lindo, Morphy & Compton (2009), _Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness_ 2(1), 1–44 **[located]**:
  37 studies, Pre-K to Grade 12. Vocabulary instruction improved comprehension of passages with taught words (d about 0.50)
  but had much less effect on standardized comprehension tests (d about 0.10). Students with reading difficulties gained more
  on researcher-made comprehension measures (d about 1.23 versus 0.39) (abstract-level figures; **verify**). Honest summary:
  teaching words helps students understand text containing those words; it does not by itself reliably lift general
  reading comprehension scores.
- Marulis & Neuman (2010), _Review of Educational Research_ 80(3), 300–335 **[located]**: 67 studies, 216 effect sizes,
  young children (preschool through early elementary); overall effect about 0.88. Larger effects with adult-delivered
  instruction, combined explicit and implicit strategies, and researcher-created measures. The authors conclude the
  interventions are not strong enough to close the gap for at-risk children. Applies to young children; do not generalize
  to adolescents.
- Gains measured on researcher-made tests of taught words are systematically larger than gains on standardized measures
  (a pattern repeated across these reviews). The program must not market researcher-made-test-style gains as general
  reading gains.

**Morphology instruction.**

- Bowers, Kirby & Deacon (2010), _Review of Educational Research_ 80(2), 144–179 **[located]**: 22 studies, preschool to
  Grade 8. Small to moderate positive effects against untreated controls, near zero against alternative treatments;
  effects larger when morphology was combined with other literacy instruction; the authors note ability and group size
  were confounded (secondary-source figures; **verify**).
- Goodwin & Ahn (2013), _Scientific Studies of Reading_ 17(4), 257–285 **[located]**: 30 studies, moderate overall effect
  (about 0.32); significant on morphological knowledge, phonological awareness, vocabulary, decoding and spelling; not
  significant on reading comprehension or fluency; larger for younger students (abstract-level; **verify**). A 2010
  Goodwin & Ahn meta-analysis in _Annals of Dyslexia_ addressed students with literacy difficulties (overall about 0.33)
  **[located]**; do not confuse the two.
- A 2024 meta-analysis in _Educational Psychology Review_ reanalyzed morphological instruction for children in
  English-speaking countries **[located as a listing only; findings not read, verify]**.
- Honest summary: morphology instruction helps vocabulary and word-level skills; transfer to comprehension is weaker and
  uncertain; most comparisons of morphology versus another active treatment show little difference.

**Retrieval practice and spacing.**

- Roediger & Karpicke (2006), _Psychological Science_ 17(3), 249–255, "Test-enhanced learning" **[known]**: retrieval
  practice produced better delayed retention than rereading in laboratory studies with prose passages.
- Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), _Psychological Bulletin_ 132(3), 354–380 **[known]**: distributed
  practice beats massed practice across many studies; the optimal gap depends on how long retention is needed.
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), _Psychological Science in the Public Interest_ 14(1), 4–58
  **[known]**: rated practice testing and distributed practice as high-utility techniques, largely from studies with
  college students and lab materials. Applying them to K–12 classroom vocabulary is a reasonable inference, not a
  proven program effect.
- Our schedule (`INTERVAL_DAYS = [1,3,7,14,30,60]`) is a design choice informed by this literature, not a validated
  optimum.

**Vocabulary and multilingual learners.**

- August & Shanahan (Eds.) (2006), _Developing Literacy in Second-Language Learners: Report of the National Literacy Panel
  on Language-Minority Children and Youth_ **[known]**: oral English proficiency and vocabulary are weak points for
  many language-minority students; instruction should attend to vocabulary explicitly.
- Carlo, August, McLaughlin, Snow, Dressler, Lippman, Lively & White (2004), _Reading Research Quarterly_ 39(2),
  188–215 **[located]**: a fifth-grade intervention teaching useful words with strategies for context, morphology,
  multiple meanings and cognates; improved taught-word knowledge, depth, multiple meanings and comprehension; effects
  were as large for English learners as for English-only students, though English learners scored lower overall.
- Lesaux, Kieffer, Faller & Kelley (2010), _Reading Research Quarterly_ 45(2), 196–228 **[located]**: quasi-experimental
  study of an 18-week academic vocabulary program with 476 sixth graders (346 language-minority learners); gains on
  several vocabulary measures, no effect on a norm-referenced vocabulary test; the What Works Clearinghouse reported it
  did not meet its standards because of baseline equivalence. A later randomized trial exists (Lesaux and colleagues, 2014,
  _American Educational Research Journal_; **verify**).
- IES practice guide, Baker et al. (2014), _Teaching Academic Content and Literacy to English Learners in Elementary and
  Middle School_ (NCEE 2014-4012) **[known; verify details]**: recommends explicit teaching of a small set of academic
  vocabulary words across content, with multiple practice opportunities and writing and speaking use.
- **Cognates.** Spanish–English cognates are a documented asset for Spanish-speaking students; false cognates and
  differences in form and sense are a documented hazard. Specific percentages of academic words with Spanish cognates
  circulate widely; we do not state a figure (**verify** before any use).
- **Translanguaging.** García & Wei (2014) **[known]** frame the use of all of a learner's language resources as a
  legitimate learning strategy. This is a theoretical and pedagogical position with qualitative and small-scale
  support; it is not an effect-size finding for vocabulary gain. In the program it appears as optional support:
  Spanish glosses, `anyLanguageResponse`.

**WIDA English Language Development Standards Framework, 2020 Edition** **[located]**. It frames language development
through four Key Language Uses (Narrate, Inform, Explain, Argue), Language Expectations by grade cluster, and Proficiency
Level Descriptors across six levels, with listening, speaking, reading and writing domains. It is a standards framework
for planning instruction and assessment, not a vocabulary curriculum. **Vocabulary Academy is WIDA-informed only. It is
not WIDA-certified, not endorsed by WIDA, and does not generate an official score.** Copy follows the existing repo
disclaimer ("practice aligned to WIDA language domains, not an official score").

**Sentence-level writing instruction.** The Writing Revolution (Hochman & Wexler) is a practitioner method centred on
explicit sentence-level strategies (such as sentence expansion with conjunctions and combining) and paragraph outlining.
Honest status **[located]**: no randomized controlled trial was found. Evidence includes a quasi-experimental study
commissioned by the organization (not able to establish causality by its own account), an independent evaluation of
partner schools in New York with mixed results, and two small dissertation-scale experiments. Support for its components
comes indirectly from broader research on explicit strategy instruction and sentence combining. The evidence is
limited and mixed. Vocabulary Academy may borrow the general idea of sentence-level practice with target words but does
not claim to implement or validate the method.

**Other standard references used for word selection** (see `word-selection.md`) **[known]**: Coxhead (2000), _TESOL
Quarterly_ 34(2), the Academic Word List; Nagy & Townsend (2012), "Words as tools: Learning academic vocabulary as
language acquisition," _Reading Research Quarterly_ 47(1); Kuperman, Stadthagen-Gonzalez & Brysbaert (2012), age-of-
acquisition ratings for about 30,000 English words, _Behavior Research Methods_ 44(4); Brysbaert, Warriner & Kuperman (2014),
concreteness ratings, _Behavior Research Methods_ 46(3). Citation details for these four: **verify** page numbers.

## Design implications for Vocabulary Academy

1. **Teach fewer words, deeply.** 10 words per unit (5–8 in the youngest grades) with several kinds of encounter each is consistent with the case for rich, repeated instruction; breadth comes from the sequence of units and from morphology.
2. **Student-friendly definitions with explicit sense boundaries.** Definitions use simpler words, never the headword, and a second sense is taught only when genuinely different (SPEC §3.3). This follows robust instruction, not dictionary style.
3. **Pair definitions with context.** Each word gets two original example sentences, a cloze that needs a context clue, and a passage, matching the definitional-plus-contextual pattern behind the larger effects.
4. **Make every item require thinking about meaning.** Cloze items must not be answerable by grammar alone; misuse items check that students can detect wrong usage. This targets depth rather than recognition.
5. **Measure more than recognition.** Six evidence dimensions (recognition, meaning, context, morphology, production, delayed retrieval) with state derived from several kinds of evidence, never one correct answer. This responds to the repeated finding that taught-word tests overstate transfer.
6. **Space and retrieve.** Review intervals of 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 and 60 days with a missed word stepping back, plus cumulative `reviewFrom`. It is informed by spacing and retrieval research; we call it a design choice, not a proven optimum.
7. **Build morphology into the sequence, accurately.** Morphology gets its own strand and a validated dataset, linked back to unit words, with no folk etymology. Expected benefit is mainly vocabulary and word-level skills; we do not promise reading comprehension gains.
8. **Productive use is first-class.** Sentence, combining, explain and argue tasks per unit; speaking rehearsal prompts. This addresses the gap most print and adaptive products leave thin, and the sentence-level work is framed as practice, not as an evidence-validated method.
9. **Multilingual learners are designed in, not bolted on.** Spanish glosses with cognate flags (and `needsReview` where doubtful), sentence frames, word banks, oral rehearsal, any-language response, per-domain profiles. Flag false cognates and sense mismatches explicitly.
10. **WIDA-informed language, never certified.** Use Key Language Uses (narrate, inform, explain, argue) and domains to shape supports; never state a level, a score or certification.
11. **Auditable word selection.** Every word records why it is there (`src`), every grade records how words were chosen (`selection`, `why`); frequency is one input among twelve. See `word-selection.md`.
12. **Publish an honest evidence statement.** Teacher-facing documentation says what research supports each design choice, what is a design choice, and that the program itself has not been evaluated.
13. **No speed mechanics.** No timers that punish, and speed never raises score, so guessing is not rewarded and anxiety is not added; this is a design value, not a research finding.
14. **Separate teacher and student material.** Answer keys and teacher notes never appear on student routes (SPEC §8–9); this also makes unit materials safe to print.
15. **Plan for human review.** Automated checks catch structure, not correctness of meaning. Content status is labelled `auto-checked` until a person has reviewed it. See `content-qa.md`.

## Claims we will NOT make

- That Vocabulary Academy is "research-proven", "evidence-based" as a product claim, or "research proves this program works". The principles behind it are research-informed; the program has not been evaluated.
- That it is WIDA-certified, endorsed by WIDA, "predicts ACCESS", or produces an ACCESS or WIDA proficiency level.
- That it guarantees retention, mastery, test score gains, Lexile growth, or closing a vocabulary gap.
- That it improves general reading comprehension. Meta-analytic evidence shows small or uncertain transfer from vocabulary instruction to standardized comprehension.
- That Tier 2 / Tier 3 is a scientific classification, or that a word's tier is objective.
- That morphology instruction is proven to raise comprehension, or that every word has a reliable etymology.
- That The Writing Revolution is implemented, validated or endorsed here, or that sentence-level practice has proven effects in this program.
- That Spanish glosses have been reviewed by a native speaker or professional translator until that review has actually happened and is recorded.
- That it covers, replaces, matches or is aligned to any commercial program's word list, sequence or assessments.
- That its word lists are "the most important words" or "the 3,000 words you need". Lists are curated selections for instruction, not a ranking of importance.
