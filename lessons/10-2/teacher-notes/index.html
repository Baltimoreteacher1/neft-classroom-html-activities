Warm-up

This plant grows in layers, and each layer is made up of two leaves — so 3 layers hold 3 × 2 = 6 leaves.

Teaching notes

Key idea: Bilateral symmetry — two sides that are mirror images — appears throughout nature, and it shapes human creations from art to music to architecture.

I do:

  1. I look at the two sides of the butterfly, and they look like mirror images — the butterfly is symmetric.
  2. All butterflies have reflective symmetry. In plants and animals, this is called bilateral symmetry.
  3. To show it, I draw a line down the middle of the butterfly's body: everything on the left is reflected on the right.

You do: Name another animal that has reflective or bilateral symmetry. Then name one human creation — a building, a picture, a song pattern — that uses symmetry, and say where its line of symmetry is.

Common misconception

Thinking a figure lacks bilateral symmetry just because its line of symmetry is not vertical — the mirror line can run in any direction, and the test is whether the two sides match across it.

ESOL / SPED supports

  • Pre-teach: symmetric, mirror image, bilateral symmetry, line of symmetry, balance (Spanish + visuals are in the lesson vocabulary).
  • Offer the sentence frames: “I know ___ because ___.” “First, I ___. Then, I ___.” “The answer is ___, so ___.”
  • Chunk the I-do steps; let students restate each step to a partner before moving on.
  • Provide the worked example as a reference while they practice.

Reteach

Re-run the I-do example with smaller numbers and have students narrate each step using the key idea.

Extension

Your friend insists that only one of two flowers has bilateral symmetry. One flower's petals mirror each other across a line down its center; the other flower does too, just along a different line.
