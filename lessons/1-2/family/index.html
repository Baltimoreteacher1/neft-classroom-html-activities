What students are learning

The book opens with two warm-ups: about how many peas are in the picture, and decompose 105.76 five different ways. Both are about thinking before computing.

Key idea: Before solving, find what you already know and what the question is asking. Pick a plan, like drawing a picture, writing an equation, or making a table. Check how it is going, and try a new plan if you get stuck.

How we did it in class today

  1. A bowl is full of peas — far too many to count one at a time. About how many peas are in the bowl?
  2. I do not count every pea — I look for a group I CAN count, like one small cluster of about 10.
  3. Then I ask how many of those clusters would cover the whole bowl. I count about 12.
  4. 12 clusters of about 10 is 12 × 10 = 120, so my estimate is about 120 peas — a reasoned answer, close enough to be useful.

Try this together

  1. Diego is figuring out how much paint he needs for a fence. His first plan isn't working. Which approach best matches the idea that math is exploring and thinking?
    1. Do the calculation as fast as he can and move on, even if it doesn't seem right
    2. Stop and wait until someone tells him the one right way to do it
    3. Figure out what he knows and what he needs, make a plan, check how it's going, and try a new plan when he's stuck
    4. Keep going without checking, and only look back if the final number seems strange

If they get stuck...

Watch out for this: Expecting a fraction of a number to be bigger than the number — 5/6 × 540 must be LESS than 540 because 5/6 is less than 1; only a fraction greater than 1, like 6/5, makes the product bigger.

What to ask: "Can you show me how you got that? Did we miss a step?"

How families can help at home

  • Look around the house for ways to use this math together (like cooking or shopping).
  • Ask your student to teach you the example above in their own words.
  • When they get an answer, ask "Does that make sense?" before checking if it's right.
