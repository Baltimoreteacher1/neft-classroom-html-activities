Where we left off

Our goal: I can multiply decimals and place the decimal point correctly in the product, explain why the method works, and use it on a problem I have not seen before.

The big idea: Multiply like whole numbers, then the product has the SAME total of decimal places as both factors combined — and you can say why it is true, and where it would stop being true.

Model to copy — Watch me

  1. I want 4.5 × 12.60, the cost of 4.5 meters at $12.60 each.
  2. First I multiply without points, like whole numbers: 45 × 1260 = 56,700.
  3. Now I count decimal places: 4.5 has 1, and 12.60 has 2, so 1 + 2 = 3 decimal places.
  4. I put the point 3 places from the right in 56,700: that gives 56.700, or $56.70.
  5. I check with an estimate: 5 × $13 = $65, and $56.70 is close, so my answer is reasonable.

  1. 5

    Think it through3.5 × $6.80 = ?

    1. A$2.38
    2. B$21.80
    3. C$23.80
    4. D$238.00

    Why is that the answer?

    Multiply as whole numbers: 35 × 680 = 23,800. There is one decimal place in 3.5 and two in 6.80, so the product needs three: 23.800 = $23.80.

  2. 6

    Think it throughRound to estimate: about how much should the deli meat cost?

    1. Aabout $2.80
    2. Babout $14
    3. Cabout $28
    4. Dabout $280

    How do you know? Give a second reason as well.

    3.5 rounds to 4 and $6.80 rounds to $7, so 4 × 7 = $28. The exact answer $23.80 is close to $28, so it is reasonable.

  3. 7

    Think it throughA student answered $238.00. What went wrong?

    1. AThey added instead of multiplying
    2. BThey rounded too early
    3. CThey used the wrong price
    4. DThey put the decimal point in the wrong place

    Explain your thinking. What would have to change for a different choice to be right?

    35 × 680 = 23,800 is correct, but the answer needs three decimal places: $23.800 = $23.80. $238.00 is ten times too big — the $28 estimate would have caught it.

Use the words

Fill each blank with a word from the bank.

  • Count the decimal places in BOTH factors to place the ___ ___. → Multiply Decimals
  • The answer to a multiplication problem is the ___. → Product
  • The dot that separates whole numbers from parts of a whole is the ___. → Decimal point
  • Finding an answer that is close but not exact, to check if a product is reasonable, is to ___. → Estimate

Explain the thinking

  1. 9

    Find the mistakeAnother student made this exact mistake in this lesson. Explain why it is wrong, then write what they should have done.

    The mistake: A common mistake in Multiply Decimals is counting decimal places from only one factor instead of adding the decimal places from BOTH factors. For example, in 0.4 × 0.06, a student multiplies 4 × 6 = 24, then places the decimal point using just one factor's place count (1 place), writing 0.24 instead of the correct answer: 0.4 has 1 decimal place and 0.06 has 2 decimal places, for a total of 3, so 24 becomes 0.024. Before you submit, count the decimal places in EACH factor separately and add them together — that total is how many decimal places belong in your final answer.

    The full note above is the teacher's — the student sheet shows only the misconception, not the correction. A correct answer names the step that went wrong, states the rule it broke, and shows the fixed work.

    Watch for: a student who repeats the mistake back without naming why it is wrong.

    From Lesson 2.11Explain your thinking — What is 32.6 − 14.85?

    1. A17.25
    2. B17.75
    3. C17.85
    4. D18.75

    How do you know?

    Rewrite as 32.60 − 14.85. Hundredths: 0 − 5 → borrow → 10 − 5 = 5. Tenths: 5 − 8 → borrow → 15 − 8 = 7. Ones: 1 − 4 → borrow → 11 − 4 = 7. Tens: 2 − 1 = 1. Answer: 17.75.

  3. 14

    From Lesson 2.11Did she beat her personal record of 7.5 minutes?

    1. ANo, because 7.43 > 7.5
    2. BYes, because 7.43 < 7.5
    3. CNo, because 7.43 and 7.5 are equal
    4. DYou cannot compare them

    How do you know?

    7.43 is less than 7.50 (43 hundredths < 50 hundredths), and a lower time is faster — so she beat her record.
