What students are learning

The mean and the median both describe the center of a data set, but one fits better depending on the shape. When the data has an outlier (a value far from the rest), the median is usually the better choice.

Key idea: If the data has no outliers (numbers far from the rest), use the mean and the mean absolute deviation (MAD) to describe it. If the data has outliers or leans to one side, use the median and the interquartile range (IQR), because an outlier pulls the mean away from most of the numbers. To compare two data sets, look at both the typical value and the spread.
Idea clave: Si los datos no tienen valores atípicos (números muy lejos del resto), usa la media y la desviación media absoluta para describirlos. Example

  1. My data, the top scorer's points: 22, 24, 20, 25, 23, 21, 58. I notice 58 is much bigger than the rest, so it is an outlier.
  2. Mean: 22 + 24 + 20 + 25 + 23 + 21 + 58 = 193, then 193 ÷ 7 ≈ 27.6.
  3. Median: in order they are 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 58. The middle (4th) value is 23.
  4. The mean (27.6) is higher than 6 of the 7 games because 58 pulled it up. So the median (23) better shows a typical game.

Common mistake

A common mistake in Appropriate Measures is defaulting to the mean just because "it uses all the data," without first checking whether an outlier or skewed shape is present. For example, given a player's points per game 14, 16, 15, 17, 16, 58, a student calculates the mean (136 ÷ 6 ≈ 22.7) and reports that as the typical game — but 58 is far from the cluster of 14–17, so it pulls the mean above 5 of the 6 games. The median (16) actually represents a typical game here. Before choosing a measure, always scan the data for an outlier or a long tail first: only use the mean once you've confirmed the data has neither.
