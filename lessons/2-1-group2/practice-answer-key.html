Where we left off

Our goal: I can tell the difference between a statistical question and a non-statistical question, explain how I can tell, and judge a case I have not seen before.

The big idea: If you expect the answers to vary, it is a statistical question — and you can say why it is true, and where it would stop being true.

Model to copy — Watch me

  1. Question 1: "How many points did our top player score last night?" There is one player and one game, so there is one fixed answer, like 18 points.
  2. One answer, no variety, so this is NOT a statistical question.
  3. Question 2: "How many points does each player score per game?" The answers vary: 8, 12, 12, 15, 18, 20, 22.
  4. Because the answers are different for different players, I must collect data. This IS a statistical question.

    4. D40 in²

    How do you know?

    2. B30 feet

    Volume = base area × height, so the height is 40 ÷ 10 = 4 feet.

  1. 5

    Think it throughWhich question is statistical?

    1. A'How many goals did each player score this season?'
    2. B'How many goals has the team scored total?'
    3. CBoth are statistical
    4. DNeither is statistical

    Why is that the answer?

    A statistical question anticipates VARIABILITY — different answers from different players. Asking each player's goals produces many different numbers. The team total has exactly one answer.

  2. 6

    Think it throughWhy is the team total NOT a statistical question?

    1. ABecause it has exactly one answer, with no variability
    2. BBecause totals are never useful
    3. CBecause it is about soccer
    4. DBecause you cannot count goals

    How do you know? Give a second reason as well.

    Statistical questions expect a spread of answers. 'What is the total?' has a single fixed answer, so there is no distribution to describe.

  3. 7

    Think it throughWhich of these is a statistical question?

    1. AHow tall are the students in my class?
    2. BHow tall am I?
    3. CWhat is today's date?
    4. DHow many minutes are in an hour?

    Explain your thinking. What would have to change for a different choice to be right?

    Different students have different heights, so that question produces a data set with variability. Each of the others has one fixed answer.

    Look for: variability, different, one, fixed, many answers, same, varies.

Use the words

Fill each blank with a word from the bank.

  • A question that expects varied answers you can collect is a ___ ___. → Statistical Questions and Data
  • A question that expects a variety of answers and is answered by collecting data is a ___. → Statistical Question
  • A question that has one fixed answer and does not need data collection is a ___. → Non-statistical Question
  • Facts or numbers collected to answer a question are ___. → Data

Explain the thinking

  1. 9

    Find the mistakeAnother student made this exact mistake in this lesson. Explain why it is wrong, then write what they should have done.

    The mistake: A common mistake in Statistical Questions and Data is judging a question by its topic or its numbers instead of its variability. Students see a question about ONE player, ONE game, or ONE fixed fact — like "How many points does the team need to win a game?" — and call it statistical just because it has a number or is about sports. Or they see a question about a single player's whole season, like "How many touchdowns did the quarterback throw this season?", and call it NOT statistical because only one player is named, forgetting that a season covers many games with a different result each time. Both errors skip the real test: will the answers actually differ from case to case (game to game, player to player)? If yes, it is a statistical question; if there is one fixed answer, it is not.

    The full note above is the teacher's — the student sheet shows only the misconception, not the correction. A correct answer names the step that went wrong, states the rule it broke, and shows the fixed work.

    Watch for: a student who repeats the mistake back without naming why it is wrong.

  2. 10

    Say moreWhile sorting the questions, what clue tells you instantly that a question will need DATA from many players to answer?

    Questions that ask about "each" player usually anticipate variability.

    Show what you knowExplain your thinking — Which question is a statistical question? A) How many students are in the school? B) How many hours does each student in 6th grade exercise per week? C) What time does school start? D) How many days are in a week?

    1. AHow many hours does each student exercise per week?
    2. BHow many students are in the school?

    4. DHow many days are in a week?

    Explain your choice.

    "How many hours does each student exercise per week?" is statistical because different students exercise different amounts — the data has variability.

  2. 13

    From Lesson 1.6Explain your thinking — Which statement best captures what this lesson means by 'Math is Ours'?

    1. AWe are a community of math thinkers and doers — we work together and on our own, showing respect for our classmates, our community, ourselves, and our math ideas
    2. BMath belongs to whoever finishes first
    3. CWorking together means one person solves while the others watch
    4. DGetting stuck means math is not for you

    How do you know?

    The summary says it directly. We are a community of math thinkers and doers. We often work together and sometimes work on our own. We show respect for our classmates, our community, ourselves, and our math ideas.

  3. 14

    From Lesson 1.6Why should the class agree on both a together-agreement and an alone-agreement?

    1. ABecause working alone matters more than working together
    2. BBecause math class includes both kinds of work, and each kind needs its own way to support the whole community
    3. CBecause agreements for working together are only for advanced students
    4. DBecause agreements about working alone don't affect other students

    How do you know?

    The class works together sometimes and alone sometimes, so agreements are needed for both to keep the whole community supported. Neither kind of work matters 'more,' agreements aren't limited by ability level, and alone-work choices like interrupting or seeking help do affect classmates.
