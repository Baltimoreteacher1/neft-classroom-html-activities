Part 1Understand the ideamodels and meaning
  1. 1Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    You read a problem and have no idea where to start. According to this lesson, what does that mean?

    1. AYou are doing math — getting stuck is part of the process, and there are strategies to try
    2. BYou are not a math person
    3. CThe problem is broken and should be skipped
    4. DYou should wait silently until someone gives you the answer

    Hint What does the lesson say sometimes happens when we do math?

    Answer: A — You are doing math — getting stuck is part of the process, and there are strategies to try

    Why: When we do math, sometimes we get stuck — and we can try different strategies to get moving again.

    Watch for: Treating 'stuck' as a stop sign instead of a signal — waiting silently instead of trying a strategy: asking a question, drawing the problem, or recalling a similar problem.

  2. 2Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    A classmate shares an idea you think is wrong. What does our community do?

    1. ASay 'that's wrong' and move on
    2. BStay silent so nobody's feelings are hurt
    3. CLaugh, because wrong answers are funny
    4. DCritique the idea respectfully — not the classmate

    Hint What do we critique — the person or the idea?

    Answer: D — Critique the idea respectfully — not the classmate

    Why: We critique the ideas of others, not our classmates — respectfully examining an idea is how the whole community grows.

    Watch for: Treating 'stuck' as a stop sign instead of a signal — waiting silently instead of trying a strategy: asking a question, drawing the problem, or recalling a similar problem.

  3. 3Write the name of the correct group on each line.

    Sort each behavior by its effect on our math community.

    Groups: Builds our community Hurts our community
    • Builds our communityAsking a partner to explain their strategy
    • Builds our communityComparing two different approaches to the same problem
    • Builds our communitySaying 'I disagree, because...' with a reason
    • Builds our communityMaking sure every group member gets to talk
    • Hurts our communityOne student doing all the talking
    • Hurts our communityCalling an answer 'wrong' with no reason
    • Hurts our communityKeeping your strategy secret so you finish first
    • Hurts our communityLaughing when someone makes a mistake

    Hint Community behaviors invite more voices in; the others shut voices out.

    Why: Community builders share reasoning and make room for every voice — asking, comparing, disagreeing with reasons, and inviting quiet members in. The hurtful behaviors do the opposite: one voice dominates, judgments come without reasons, strategies get hoarded, and mistakes get mocked instead of used as information.

Part 1Understand the ideamodels and meaning
  1. 1Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    You are solving a problem and realize your strategy is not making progress. What does the problem-solving process say to do?

    1. AKeep using the same strategy no matter what
    2. BNotice your progress has stalled and shift to a different strategy
    3. CErase everything and decide the problem is impossible
    4. DCopy a classmate's paper

    Answer: B — Notice your progress has stalled and shift to a different strategy

    Why: We are aware of our progress in solving the problem and shift strategies when needed.

    Watch for: Treating 'stuck' as a stop sign instead of a signal — waiting silently instead of trying a strategy: asking a question, drawing the problem, or recalling a similar problem.

  2. 2Complete the table. Show how you found each value.

    Use the bicycle rack problem to complete the table.

    QuestionThinkingAnswer
    Wheels on a rack of 8 bikes
    Wheels on the bigger rack (6 times as many)
    Bikes on the bigger rack

    Why: 8 bikes × 2 wheels = 16 wheels. The bigger rack has 6 times as many: 16 × 6 = 96 wheels. Dividing by 2 wheels per bike returns to bikes: 96 ÷ 2 = 48. Explaining each step — not just the final 48 — is what this lesson calls precise mathematical language.

Part 2Apply itreal-world problems
  1. 3Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    The first rack has 16 wheels and the other rack has 6 times as many. How many BIKES does the bigger rack hold, if every bike has 2 wheels?

    1. A24
    2. B48
    3. C96
    4. D192

    Answer: B — 48

    Why: The bigger rack has 16 × 6 = 96 wheels, and 96 ÷ 2 = 48 bikes.

    Watch for: Treating 'stuck' as a stop sign instead of a signal — waiting silently instead of trying a strategy: asking a question, drawing the problem, or recalling a similar problem.

  2. 4Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    You are working on your own and get stuck. Which move follows BOTH parts of our working-alone agreements?

    1. AImmediately call across the room to a friend
    2. BSit silently for the rest of class without seeking help
    3. CTake a classmate's paper to see their work
    4. DTry a get-unstuck strategy first, then quietly ask for help without interrupting classmates unnecessarily

    Answer: D — Try a get-unstuck strategy first, then quietly ask for help without interrupting classmates unnecessarily

    Why: When we work on our own, we seek help when we are stuck AND we avoid interrupting our classmates unnecessarily — the best move honors both.

    Watch for: Treating 'stuck' as a stop sign instead of a signal — waiting silently instead of trying a strategy: asking a question, drawing the problem, or recalling a similar problem.

Part 3Explain and correctreasoning
  1. 5Answer in complete sentences.

    Describe your problem-solving process in order, from reading the problem to checking your answer. Use at least three steps from this lesson.

    Sample answer: First I make sense of the problem — what I know and what I don't. Then I look for patterns and choose a representation, like a drawing or a table. I make a plan and carry it out, watching whether I'm making progress. If I stall, I shift strategies. At the end I check whether my answer makes sense for the question.

  2. 6Answer in complete sentences.

    What part of problem solving is easy for you? What part is challenging?

    Sample answer: Making sense of the problem is easy for me — I can usually restate what it's asking. Shifting strategies is challenging, because once I start one way I don't like abandoning it, even when it stalls.

  3. 7Answer in complete sentences.

    What skills and knowledge do you bring to a group that help the group be successful?

    Sample answer: I'm good at drawing a problem so everyone can see it, and I ask questions when something is unclear, which usually helps someone else who was confused too.

Lesson 1.6Math is Ours

Challenge · Answer KeyExtension

Learning target I can describe my problem-solving process, name strategies for getting unstuck, and identify the behaviors that make our class a community of math thinkers.

Part 1Understand the ideamodels and meaning
  1. 1Write the letter of the matching item on each line.

    Match each stuck moment to the strategy that gets you unstuck.

    1. AMy strategy is not making progress
    2. BI don't understand the problem
    3. CMy answer looks unreasonable
    4. DMy partner got a different answer
    • ATry a different entry point
    • BRestate it in your own words
    • CCheck it against an estimate
    • DCompare steps and find where they differ

    Why: The problem-solving process pairs each kind of stuck with its own move: a stalled strategy needs a new entry point; a confusing problem needs restating; a suspicious answer needs an estimate check; and disagreeing answers need a step-by-step comparison to find where the paths split. Knowing WHICH move fits is what makes the process a tool.

Part 2Apply itreal-world problems
  1. 2Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    A group solves a hard problem, but one student did all the talking while three stayed silent. By this lesson's standards, how did the GROUP do?

    1. APerfectly — the answer was right, and that is all that matters
    2. BNot well — the talker should have stayed silent too
    3. CPerfectly — silent members were being respectful
    4. DNot well — a community shares thinking and takes turns, so three voices went unheard

    Answer: D — Not well — a community shares thinking and takes turns, so three voices went unheard

    Why: We share our thinking and take turns when sharing ideas — a right answer that silences three thinkers costs the community three sets of ideas.

    Watch for: Treating 'stuck' as a stop sign instead of a signal — waiting silently instead of trying a strategy: asking a question, drawing the problem, or recalling a similar problem.

Part 3Explain and correctreasoning
  1. 3Answer in complete sentences.

    Write THREE community agreements you would propose for our class — at least one for working together and one for working alone — and for each, say what it protects.

    Sample answer: 1) We critique ideas, not people — it protects everyone's courage to share unfinished thinking. 2) We take turns when sharing — it protects the quieter voices that often see what others miss. 3) When working alone, we seek help when stuck but ask quietly — it protects both the stuck student's progress and everyone else's focus.

  2. 4Answer in complete sentences.

    Solve this with your full process, narrating each step: a rack has 16 wheels, and another rack has 6 times as many wheels. If a third rack held HALF as many wheels as the big rack, how many bikes would fit on it?

    Sample answer: Make sense: I know 16 wheels and '6 times as many'; I need bikes on a third rack. Plan: big rack is 16 × 6 = 96 wheels; half of 96 is 48 wheels; each bike has 2 wheels, so 48 ÷ 2 = 24 bikes. Check: 24 bikes × 2 wheels = 48 wheels, which is half of 96. The steps agree, so 24 bikes is reasonable.

  3. 5Answer in complete sentences.

    A classmate says: 'Math class would be better with no group work — thinking is private.' Using this lesson, write a respectful critique of that IDEA.

    Sample answer: I hear the idea that focus matters, and the lesson agrees — we sometimes work on our own, staying focused and respecting boundaries. But the lesson also says math is done with others: we listen, share thinking, and critique ideas so everyone grows. A class with only private thinking loses the moment when someone else's strategy unsticks you. So I'd critique the 'no group work' part while keeping the respect for quiet focus.
