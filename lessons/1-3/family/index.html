How we did it in class today

  1. A pile of coins is too big to count one at a time. About how many coins are in the pile?
  2. I do not count every coin — I find a small stack I CAN count, about 10 coins.
  3. Then I estimate how many stacks like that are in the pile. I count about 15.
  4. 15 stacks of about 10 is 15 × 10 = 150, so my estimate is about 150 coins — reasoned, not random.

Try this together

  1. Lena is planning snacks and drinks for her soccer team's end-of-season party. Which way of working best matches the idea that math is in our world?
    1. Type every number into a calculator, because real-life problems can't be solved any other way
    2. Solve it first, then draw a table at the end just to make the page look nice
    3. Use a tape diagram for every problem she ever solves, whether it fits or not
    4. Picture the party, use a table or diagram to show how the amounts connect, and choose the tool that fits best

If they get stuck...

Watch out for this: Converting minutes to hours by moving the decimal point — 625 minutes is NOT 6.25 hours. An hour is 60 minutes, so divide by 60: 625 ÷ 60 ≈ 10.42 hours.

What to ask: "Can you show me how you got that? Did we miss a step?"

How families can help at home

  • Look around the house for ways to use this math together (like cooking or shopping).
  • Ask your student to teach you the example above in their own words.
  • When they get an answer, ask "Does that make sense?" before checking if it's right.
