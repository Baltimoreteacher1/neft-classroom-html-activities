Start here pageNow you try — model solution

  1. Double the number of steps and add 1: 1 doubled plus 1 is 3, and 3 doubled plus 1 is 7.
  2. So 4 discs take 7 × 2 + 1 = 15 steps, and 5 discs take 15 × 2 + 1 = 31 steps.
  3. The pattern just solved a puzzle we never touched.
Part 1Understand the ideamodels and meaning
  1. 1Write the name of the correct group on each line.

    Sort each move: legal or against the rules?

    Groups: Legal move Against the rules
    • Legal moveMove the top disc of any stack
    • Legal movePlace a small disc on a bigger disc
    • Legal moveMove exactly one disc at a time
    • Against the rulesMove two discs at once
    • Against the rulesPlace a big disc on a smaller disc
    • Against the rulesPull a disc out from the middle of a stack

    Hint Only the TOP disc of a stack can move, and only one at a time.

    Why: The three legal moves are the whole rule set: take only a top disc, move one at a time, and rest smaller on bigger. The illegal moves each break exactly one rule — two at once, big on small, or grabbing from mid-stack. Every 2ⁿ − 1 solution is built from nothing but the legal three.

    3
    4

    Why: The recursion is the reason for the rule: to move n discs you solve n − 1 discs (7 moves), carry the biggest disc (1 move), and solve n − 1 again (7 moves) — so 4 discs need 15, 5 need 31, and 6 need 2 × 31 + 1 = 63. The pattern 1, 3, 7, 15, 31, 63 is one less than the doubling numbers 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64.

Part 2Apply itreal-world problems
  1. 2Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    The table shows 1 disc → 1 step, 2 discs → 3 steps, 3 discs → 7 steps. What is the pattern rule for the number of steps when you add one more disc?

    1. AAdd 2 to the number of steps
    2. BAdd the number of discs to the steps
    3. CMultiply the number of steps by 3
    4. DDouble the number of steps and add 1

    Answer: D — Double the number of steps and add 1

    Why: 1 × 2 + 1 = 3 and 3 × 2 + 1 = 7, so each new disc doubles the steps and adds 1.

    Watch for: Doubling the previous number of steps but forgetting to add 1 — answering 14 instead of 15 for four discs, or 30 instead of 31 for five.

Part 1Understand the ideamodels and meaning
  1. 1Write the letter of the matching item on each line.

    Match each tower to the calculation that gives its fewest moves.

    1. A4-disc tower
    2. B5-disc tower
    3. C6-disc tower
    4. DAny n-disc tower
    • A2 × 7 + 1 = 15
    • B2 × 15 + 1 = 31
    • C2 × 31 + 1 = 63
    • DDouble the (n − 1)-disc answer, add 1

    Why: The chain 7 → 15 → 31 → 63 applies double-plus-one three times, and the fourth card names the general rule that generates the whole sequence. Recognizing a specific pattern AND its general statement as the same idea is the heart of algorithmic thinking in this lesson.

Part 2Apply itreal-world problems
  1. 2Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    A five-disc tower takes 31 steps. Using the pattern rule, how many steps would a SIX-disc tower take?

    1. A32 steps
    2. B36 steps
    3. C62 steps
    4. D63 steps

    Answer: D — 63 steps

    Why: Apply the rule to 31: double it and add 1, so 31 × 2 + 1 = 63 steps.

    Watch for: Doubling the previous number of steps but forgetting to add 1 — answering 14 instead of 15 for four discs, or 30 instead of 31 for five.

  2. 3Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    Look at the table a different way: 1 disc → 1, 2 discs → 3, 3 discs → 7, 4 discs → 15, 5 discs → 31. Which describes the relationship between the number of discs and the number of steps?

    1. AThe steps are the number of discs multiplied by itself
    2. BThe steps are 6 times the number of discs, plus or minus 1
    3. CThe steps are the two previous step counts added together
    4. DThe steps are 1 less than a doubling pattern: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 each minus 1

    Answer: D — The steps are 1 less than a doubling pattern: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 each minus 1

    Why: 2 − 1 = 1, 4 − 1 = 3, 8 − 1 = 7, 16 − 1 = 15, 32 − 1 = 31 — every entry is one less than the doubling pattern 2, 4, 8, 16, 32.

    Watch for: Doubling the previous number of steps but forgetting to add 1 — answering 14 instead of 15 for four discs, or 30 instead of 31 for five.

Part 3Explain and correctreasoning
  1. 4Answer in complete sentences.

    The 15 Puzzle is a sliding puzzle with 15 tiles in a frame 4 tiles high by 4 tiles wide. You arrange the tiles from 1 to 15 by sliding one tile at a time into the open space. Sometimes it is solvable and sometimes it is not. How would you make an argument about whether a particular 15 Puzzle is solvable, using what this lesson taught about being playful?

    Sample answer: I would play the way we played the Tower of Hanoi: start with small cases and look for patterns. The frame is 4 × 4 = 16 spaces holding 15 tiles, so there is exactly one open space to slide into. I would try to solve it, keep track of which arrangements I can reach, and look for a pattern or relationship in the tile positions that separates the solvable starting setups from the impossible ones. If every attempt at a certain arrangement fails the same way, that repeated pattern becomes my evidence for arguing it is unsolvable.

  2. 5Answer in complete sentences.

    The summary says playing with math lets us think differently by looking for patterns AND by imagining ways to visualize a problem. Describe how you used BOTH of those with the Tower of Hanoi.

    Sample answer: I used imagination first: before moving anything, I pictured the solved puzzle — the same stack, smallest on top, standing on a different rod. Then I used patterns: I organized the small cases in a table (1 disc → 1 step, 2 → 3, 3 → 7) and found the double-and-add-1 rule, which predicted 15 steps for four discs and 31 for five. Imagining gave me the target, and the pattern gave me the count — together they solved towers I never touched.
