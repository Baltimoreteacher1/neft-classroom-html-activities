  1. 1

    Tonight the Ferris wheel has 19 cars, and about 4 people fit in each car. Mia rounds 19 up to 20 and says, “About 80 people can ride at one time.” Is Mia's estimate reasonable?

    1. AYes — 20 is close to 19, and 20 × 4 = 80 is close to the exact 76
    2. BNo — she has to use 19, so 76 is the only answer allowed
    3. CNo — rounding up always makes an estimate too big to be useful
    4. DYes, but only because 80 is a round number

    19 × 4 = 76 and 20 × 4 = 80. Mia's estimate is 4 riders high — close enough to plan with, which is what an estimate is for.

    Watch for: Believing that 'doing math' only counts when it happens on paper in a classroom.

  2. 2

    You want to estimate 19 × 4 in your head. Which move makes the numbers friendlier without changing the answer much?

    1. ARound 19 to 20 — then 20 × 4 is a double of a double
    2. BRound 4 up to 10, because tens are the easiest to multiply
    3. CAdd 19 + 4 first, then multiply the total by 2
    4. DRewrite 19 as 19.0 so it looks like a decimal

    20 is one step from 19, and 20 × 4 is easy: double 20 to 40, double again to 80. Moving 4 up to 10 would more than double the riders in every car.

    Watch for: Believing that 'doing math' only counts when it happens on paper in a classroom.

  4. 4

    A different wheel has 22 cars holding about 4 people each. Ana rounds 22 down to 20 and says “about 80.” Ben multiplies 22 × 4 and says “exactly 88.” Which statement describes both approaches fairly?

    1. AAna's is fast for a quick head count; Ben's is what the operator needs to sell tickets
    2. BAna is wrong, because she changed a number that was given
    3. CBen is wrong, because an estimate has to be a round number
    4. DOnly one of them can be doing real math

    22 × 4 = 88, and 20 × 4 = 80. Both are good mathematics doing different jobs: Ana's is quick and close, Ben's is exact. Choosing between them is a decision about what the number is for.

    Watch for: Believing that 'doing math' only counts when it happens on paper in a classroom.

  5. 5

    A third student looks at the same 22-car wheel and says, “About 200 people can ride at one time.” Why is that estimate not reasonable?

    1. AIt would mean about 9 people squeezed into every car, not about 4
    2. BIt is wrong only because 200 is not a multiple of 4
    3. CIt is fine — an estimate can be any number you like
    4. DIt is wrong because 200 is too round to be an estimate

    200 riders shared across 22 cars is about 9 per car (200 ÷ 22 ≈ 9), and the cars hold about 4. Checking an estimate against the quantity it came from is how you catch one that has drifted.

    Watch for: Believing that 'doing math' only counts when it happens on paper in a classroom.
