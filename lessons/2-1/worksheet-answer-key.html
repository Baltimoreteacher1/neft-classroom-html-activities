    Match each question to its type.

    1. AHow tall is each swimmer on the team?
    2. BWhat color is the team mascot?
    3. CHow many laps does each runner complete?
    4. DHow many players are on a soccer team?
    • AStatistical
    • BNot Statistical
    • CStatistical
    • DNot Statistical

    Hint Read each question on the left and underline the word 'each' when you see it — it's a clue that many people are being measured.

    Why: A question is statistical when the answers are expected to differ from person to person, and non-statistical when there is one fixed answer for everyone.

  2. 2Write the name of the correct group on each line.

    Sort each question by type.

    Groups: Statistical Question Not a Statistical Question
    • Statistical QuestionHow many books did each student read this month?
    • Statistical QuestionWhat grade did each student earn on the test?
    • Not a Statistical QuestionHow many days are in a week?
    • Not a Statistical QuestionWhat is the school mascot?

    Hint Statistical questions expect different answers from different people or observations.

    Why: The books-read and test-grade questions ask about each student, so different students give different answers — that variability is what makes a question statistical. The number of days in a week and the school mascot each have one fixed answer that never changes, so they are not statistical.

Part 2Apply itreal-world problems
  1. 3Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    Which of the following is a statistical question?

    1. AHow many points did each player score this season?
    2. BHow many points are scored for a free throw?
    3. CWhat sport does this team play?
    4. DHow many halves are in a soccer game?

    Hint Re-read each question and ask: would different people or games give different answers, or is there just one fixed answer?

    Answer: A — How many points did each player score this season?

    Why: "How many points did each player score?" is statistical because different players will have different scores — there is variability in the data.

    Watch for: A common mistake in Statistical Questions and Data is judging a question by its topic or its numbers instead of its variability. Students see a question about ONE player, ONE game, or ONE fixed fact — like "How many points does the team need to win a game?" — and call it statistical just because it has a number or is about sports. Or they see a question about a single player's whole season, like "How many touchdowns did the quarterback throw this season?", and call it NOT statistical because only one player is named, forgetting that a season covers many games with a different result each time. Both errors skip the real test: will the answers actually differ from case to case (game to game, player to player)? If yes, it is a statistical question; if there is one fixed answer, it is not.

    Spot the Common Mistake

    1. 1QuestionHow tall is each player on the basketball team?
    2. 2Student decisionThis is NOT a statistical question.
    3. 3Student reasonHeight is just one number, so every answer will be the same.

    Which step has the mistake? Explain what went wrong, then show the correct work.

    Hint The word "each" is a clue that you need data from many players.

    Mistake: Step 3. The reasoning step claims every answer will be the same, but the question asks about EACH player and players have different heights — 58 in, 60 in, 63 in, 65 in. Because the answers vary from player to player, the question is statistical after all.

    Correct work: The reason is wrong. The question asks about EACH player, and different players have different heights — for example 58 in, 60 in, 63 in, 65 in. Because the answers vary from player to player, this IS a statistical question.
