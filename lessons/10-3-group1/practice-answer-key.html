Where we left off

Our goal: With my small group, I can use patterns and relationships to make sense of the Tower of Hanoi puzzle and think logically about its solutions — one step at a time, with support.

The big idea: We play with math by using our imagination and by looking for patterns or relationships that help us think logically — a pattern can tell us the answer to a puzzle we have not even played yet.

Model to copy — Watch me imagine the solution first

  1. Before moving anything, I describe what the solved puzzle will look like: the discs in the same order — smallest on top to largest on bottom — but on a different rod.
  2. Then I play the small cases: a one-disc tower takes 1 move, and a two-disc tower takes 3 moves.
  3. A three-disc tower takes at least 7 moves. Small cases are how mathematicians play.

    Let's organize the moves in a table: Discs: 1, 2, 3 — Steps: 1, 3, 7. What is happening from each row to the next? Double the number of steps and add 1: 1 doubled plus 1 is 3, and 3 doubled plus 1 is 7. So 4 discs take 7 × 2 + 1 = 15 steps, and 5 discs take 15 × 2 + 1 = 31 steps.   1. 5

    Think it throughThe table shows: 1 disc = 1 step, 2 discs = 3 steps, 3 discs = 7 steps. What is the pattern rule from one row to the next?

    1. AAdd 2 to the previous number of steps
    2. BDouble the previous number of steps and add 1
    3. CMultiply the previous number of steps by 3
    4. DAdd the disc number to the previous steps

    Why is that the answer?

    I chose ___ because ___ .

    1 × 2 + 1 = 3 and 3 × 2 + 1 = 7, so the rule is double and add 1. Adding 2 matches 1→3 but fails on 3→5≠7, multiplying by 3 matches 1→3 but fails on 3→9≠7, and adding the disc number matches 1+2=3 but fails on 3+3=6≠7.

  2. 6

    Think it throughUsing the rule "double the steps and add 1," how many steps does a 4-disc tower take, starting from the 3-disc value of 7 steps?

    1. A8
    2. B14
    3. C15
    4. D21

    How do you know?

    I chose ___ because ___ .

    7 × 2 + 1 = 15. 8 comes from only adding 1 without doubling, 14 comes from only doubling without adding 1, and 21 comes from tripling instead of doubling.

  3. 7

    Think it throughContinuing the pattern from 15 steps for a 4-disc tower, how many steps does a 5-disc tower take?

    1. A16
    2. B30
    3. C31
    4. D45

    Explain your thinking.

    I chose ___ because ___ .

    15 × 2 + 1 = 31. 16 comes from only adding 1, 30 comes from only doubling, and 45 comes from tripling 15 instead of doubling and adding 1.

  4. 8

    Back to the modelA one-disc Tower of Hanoi takes 1 move, a two-disc tower takes 3 moves, and a three-disc tower takes 7 moves. What pattern rule takes you from one row to the next?

    The pattern rule is to ___ the number of moves and then ___.

    The pattern rule is to double the previous number of moves and add 1: 1 × 2 + 1 = 3, and 3 × 2 + 1 = 7.

  1. 9

    Find the mistakeAnother student made this exact mistake on this lesson. Explain why it is wrong, then write what they should have done.

    The mistake: Doubling the previous number of steps but forgetting to add 1 — answering 14 instead of 15 for four discs, or 30 instead of 31 for five.

    The full note above is the teacher's — the student sheet shows only the misconception, not the correction. A correct answer names the step that went wrong, states the rule it broke, and shows the fixed work.

    Watch for: a student who repeats the mistake back without naming why it is wrong.

  2. 10

    Say moreWhen you sorted moves into 'legal move' and 'breaks a rule,' what made 'placing a smaller disc on top of a larger disc' legal but the reverse illegal?

    Smaller on larger is legal because ___.

    The rule is that a larger disc can never sit on top of a smaller disc — the reverse, smaller on larger, is always allowed.

  3. 14

    From Lesson 10.2Your friend says only one of two flowers has bilateral symmetry. You test both by drawing a line through each flower's center and find both sides match on both flowers. What is most likely true?

    1. AThe friend is right — only one flower truly has symmetry
    2. BBoth flowers have bilateral symmetry, but the second flower's line of symmetry likely runs in a direction the friend didn't try
    3. CSymmetry cannot be tested in flowers
    4. DThe two flowers must be identical in shape to both be symmetric

    Since the test showed both flowers have a line where the sides mirror each other, both are bilaterally symmetric — the friend likely only checked one direction on the second flower and missed its line of symmetry. Flowers don't need to look alike to each have their own symmetry.
