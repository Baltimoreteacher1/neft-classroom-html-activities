Learning target I can find the greatest common factor and the least common multiple of two numbers, and use each to solve a real problem.

Part 1Understand the ideamodels and meaning
  1. 1Write the name of the correct group on each line.

    Sort each number by its relationship to 10.

    Groups: A factor of 10 A multiple of 10
    • A factor of 102
    • A multiple of 1020
    • A factor of 105
    • A multiple of 1030
    • A factor of 1010
    • A multiple of 1050

    Hint Ask: does this number divide 10, or does 10 divide it?

    Why: Factors divide 10 evenly and are never larger than 10. Multiples are what 10 builds and are never smaller than 10. The number 10 is both a factor and a multiple of itself.

Part 2Apply itreal-world problems
  1. 2Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    Which list shows all the factors of 12?

    1. A1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
    2. B1, 12
    3. C2, 3, 4, 6
    4. D12, 24, 36, 48

    Hint Ask: what divides 12 with nothing left over?

    Answer: A — 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12

    Why: A factor divides 12 evenly. Every one of 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12 does, and nothing else does.

    Watch for: A common mistake is reaching for the wrong tool: listing FACTORS for a problem about repeating cycles. Ayasha's classes repeat every 6 and 8 weeks, so the weeks they land on are multiples — answering with the GCF of 2 claims a class that meets every 8 weeks happens in week 2. Use the size of the answer as a check: a GCF is never larger than the smaller number, and an LCM is never smaller than the larger one.

    Hint Skip-count by 8 out loud.

    Answer: C — 8, 16, 24, 32

    Why: Multiples come from multiplying by 1, 2, 3, 4: 8, 16, 24, and 32 — the numbers you say skip-counting by 8.

    Answer: C — 6

Part 3Explain and correctreasoning
  1. 6Find the mistake. Explain it, then show the correct work.

    Find the error

    1. 1ProblemFind the GCF of 12 and 18.
    2. 2Step 1Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
    3. 3Step 2Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18
    4. 4Step 3The GCF is 36, because 36 is in both times tables.

    Which step has the mistake? Explain what went wrong, then show the correct work.

    Hint Compare Step 3's answer to the two numbers themselves.

    Mistake: Step 4. A factor can never be larger than the number it divides. Any answer bigger than 12 or 18 must be a multiple, which is the fastest way to catch this swap.

    Correct work: Step 3 names a common MULTIPLE, not a common factor. The common factors are 1, 2, 3, and 6, so the GCF is 6.

    Sort each real problem by the tool that solves it.

    Groups: Use the GCF Use the LCM
    • Use the GCFSplit 48 pencils and 36 notebooks into identical packs
    • Use the LCMTwo classes repeat every 6 and 8 weeks — when do they meet?

    • Use the LCMThe answer is at least the larger number

    Why: Splitting an existing amount into equal groups is a factor question; waiting for repeating cycles to line up is a multiple question. The size of the answer is a useful check on which one you used.

Part 2Apply itreal-world problems
  1. 3Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    Using prime factors, 48 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 and 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3. What is their GCF?

    1. A2, the only prime they share
    2. B6, from one 2 and one 3
    3. C12, from the shared 2 × 2 × 3
    4. D144, from multiplying all the primes

    Answer: C — 12, from the shared 2 × 2 × 3

    Why: Take only the primes both numbers have: two 2s and one 3. Multiplying them gives 2 × 2 × 3 = 12, matching the answer from the factor lists.

    Watch for: A common mistake is reaching for the wrong tool: listing FACTORS for a problem about repeating cycles. Use the size of the answer as a check: a GCF is never larger than the smaller number, and an LCM is never smaller than the larger one.

  2. 4Circle the letter of the best answer. Show how you know.

    Ayasha has painting every 8 weeks and pottery every 6 weeks, both this week. When do they next fall in the same week?

    1. AIn 2 weeks
    2. BIn 14 weeks
    3. CIn 24 weeks
    4. DIn 48 weeks

    Answer: C — In 24 weeks

    Why: Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24 …; multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24 …. The first week they share is week 24.

Part 3Explain and correctreasoning
  1. 5Find the mistake. Explain it, then show the correct work.

    Find the error

    1. 1ProblemAyasha has painting every 8 weeks and pottery every 6 weeks. When do both fall in the same week?
    2. 2Step 1Factors of 8: 1, 2, 4, 8
    3. 3Step 2Factors of 6: 1, 2, 3, 6
    4. 4Step 3The greatest common factor is 2, so both classes meet in week 2.

    Which step has the mistake? Explain what went wrong, then show the correct work.

    Mistake: Step 2. The answer is also unreasonable on its face: a class that meets every 8 weeks cannot possibly happen in week 2. Checking the answer against the story catches the wrong tool immediately.

    Correct work: Step 1 starts down the wrong road: the classes REPEAT, so the weeks they land on are multiples, not factors. List multiples of 8 (8, 16, 24 …) and of 6 (6, 12, 18, 24 …); the first shared week is 24.

    Find both the GCF and the LCM of each pair.

    NumbersGCFLCM
    6 and 8224
    12 and 18

Part 2Explain and correctreasoning
  1. 2Find the mistake. Explain it, then show the correct work.

    Find the error

    1. 1ProblemFind the LCM of 6 and 8.
    2. 2Step 1Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48
    3. 3Step 2Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48
    4. 4Step 3Both lists contain 48, so the LCM is 48.

    Which step has the mistake? Explain what went wrong, then show the correct work.

    Mistake: Step 4. Two numbers have infinitely many common multiples. The word LEAST is doing real work in the name, and scanning the lists from the start rather than the end is what finds it.

    Correct work: Step 3 picks a common multiple, but not the LEAST one. Both lists also contain 24, and 24 comes first — so the LCM is 24. (48 is the product 6 × 8, which is always A common multiple, just rarely the smallest.)

  2. 3Answer in complete sentences.

    Reginald is having hot dogs for 24 guests and wants the same number of hot dogs and buns, with none left over. Hot dogs come in packs of 10 and buns in packs of 8. How many packs of each should he buy, and how many will he have? Explain which tool you used and why.

    Sample answer: This is a least-common-multiple problem: the pack sizes repeat, and he needs the first count both can reach exactly. Multiples of 10: 10, 20, 30, 40 …; multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40 …. The first number in both lists is 40, so the LCM of 10 and 8 is 40. That means 4 packs of hot dogs (4 × 10 = 40) and 5 packs of buns (5 × 8 = 40), giving 40 of each with nothing left over. It is a multiple problem rather than a factor one because nothing is being split into groups — he is counting packs forward until the two totals match. He will have 40 of each for 24 guests, which is more than enough; buying fewer packs would leave hot dogs or buns unmatched.
