Where we left off

Our goal: With my small group, I can represent a real-world situation with a tape diagram or table and use decimal operations to solve it — one step at a time, with support.

The big idea: When we do math, we visualize and represent problems, use tools to show relationships among quantities, and make strategic decisions about which tool fits the problem.

Model to copy — Watch me estimate the coins

  1. A pile of coins is too big to count one at a time. About how many coins are in the pile?
  2. I do not count every coin — I find a small stack I CAN count, about 10 coins.
  3. Then I estimate how many stacks like that are in the pile. I count about 15.
  4. 15 stacks of about 10 is 15 × 10 = 150, so my estimate is about 150 coins — reasoned, not random.

  1. 5

    Think it throughThe tram problem asks how many round trips fit in 10 hours. What is the FIRST thing you must find before you can read the answer off your table?

    1. AHow many minutes are in 10 hours, so the units match the table
    2. BHow many miles the tram travels in total
    3. CHow many passengers ride each trip
    4. DThe price of a ticket

    Why is that the answer?

    I chose ___ because ___ .

    The table is built in minutes, but the question gives hours. Convert first: 10 hours = 600 minutes. Now you can find where 600 falls among the table's rows.

  2. 6

    Think it throughUsing the table (35→1, 175→5, 350→10, 525→15, 700→20 minutes→trips), why does the answer end up being 17 round trips, not 18?

    1. A17 trips take 595 minutes, which fits within 600, but an 18th trip would need 630 minutes, past the limit
    2. B18 trips is closer to 600 minutes, so it is the better estimate
    3. CA round trip can be counted as complete even if it is only partly finished
    4. DThe table only lists rows up to 20, so 17 is the safe middle choice

    How do you know?

    I chose ___ because ___ .

    Try 17 trips: 17 × 35 = 595 minutes. That is still inside the 600-minute limit. Try 18: 18 × 35 = 630 minutes, which goes past it. A round trip only counts once it is fully complete.

  3. 7

    Think it through600 ÷ 35 ≈ 17.14. Why is it risky to round this quotient down to 17 WITHOUT also checking the table?

    1. ARounding down happens to work here, but only the table confirms 17 × 35 stays under 600 while 18 × 35 does not
    2. BRounding down never works for time problems
    3. C17.14 should round up to 18 because .14 rounds upward
    4. DThe table and division always disagree with each other

    Explain your thinking.

    I chose ___ because ___ .

    600 ÷ 35 ≈ 17.14. The .14 left over is an unfinished trip, not extra completed ones. Rounding alone does not prove that. Checking the table's real minute totals is what shows 17 is right.

    I would ___ 4,000 by 80 to find the number of rides.

    Moving 4,000 passengers in rides of 80 each requires dividing 4,000 by 80.

Explain the thinking

  1. 9

    Find the mistakeAnother student made this exact mistake on this lesson. Explain why it is wrong, then write what they should have done.

    The mistake: Converting minutes to hours by moving the decimal point — 625 minutes is NOT 6.25 hours. An hour is 60 minutes, so divide by 60: 625 ÷ 60 ≈ 10.42 hours.

    The full note above is the teacher's — the student sheet shows only the misconception, not the correction. A correct answer names the step that went wrong, states the rule it broke, and shows the fixed work.

    Watch for: a student who repeats the mistake back without naming why it is wrong.

  2. 10

    Say moreYou sorted 'find how many rides are needed for 4,000 passengers' under division and 'find the total minutes for 50 rides at 12.5 minutes each' under multiplication. What tells you which operation a tram task needs?

    This task needs ___ because we are ___.

    Some tram tasks split a total into equal groups (division), while others combine equal groups into a total (multiplication).

  2. 13

    From Lesson 1.2Quick check — you've got this: Which statement best shows what this lesson means by "math is exploring and thinking"?

    1. AWe make sense of problems, plan a strategy, check our progress, and try other strategies at dead ends
    2. BWe compute as fast as possible and move on to the next problem
    3. CWe wait for someone to show us the one correct method
    4. DWe only check our work when the answer looks wrong

    The summary names a whole cycle, not one step. First make sense of the problem. Then develop a plan and check your progress as you go. Finally make sense of the solution in context.

  3. 14

    From Lesson 1.2Building height problem: which statement shows you are CHECKING your solution against the relationship in the problem?

    1. A“5/6 is less than 1, so my answer for London should be less than 540 meters”
    2. B“My answer is 650 meters, and that seems like a big number so it's probably right”
    3. C“I don't need to check once I've multiplied”
    4. D“Since I used a fraction, the answer must be exact”

    Checking means testing your answer against the relationship in the problem. Here the fraction is less than 1, so the result must be less than 540. That is the check. Judging whether a number looks big is not.
