  1. 1

    The assembly line produces 2.5 cars per hour. How many cars does it produce in one 8-hour shift?

    1. A20 cars
    2. B10.5 cars
    3. C16 cars
    4. D25 cars

    Substitute 8 hours: 2.5 × 8 = 20 cars per shift.

    Watch for: Adding the rate instead of multiplying — writing 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 cars for an 8-hour shift instead of 2.5 × 8 = 20. The rate applies to EVERY hour, so the total is always rate × amount.

  2. 2

    Let h = hours of operation and c = cars produced. Which equation represents the assembly line?

    1. Ac = 2.5h
    2. Bc = 2.5 + h
    3. Ch = 2.5c
    4. Dc = h ÷ 2.5

    Every hour the plant produces 2.5 cars, so the total is 2.5 times the hours: c = 2.5h. Check with the table: 8 hours gives 20 cars.

    Watch for: Adding the rate instead of multiplying — writing 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 cars for an 8-hour shift instead of 2.5 × 8 = 20. The rate applies to EVERY hour, so the total is always rate × amount.

  3. 3

    Using the table (1 hour, 2.5 cars; 8 hours, 20 cars; 16 hours, 40 cars), how many cars are produced in 24 hours?

    1. A60 cars
    2. B26.5 cars
    3. C40 cars
    4. D50 cars

    24 hours at 2.5 cars per hour is 2.5 × 24 = 60 cars — three full shifts of 20.

    Watch for: Adding the rate instead of multiplying — writing 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 cars for an 8-hour shift instead of 2.5 × 8 = 20. The rate applies to EVERY hour, so the total is always rate × amount.

  4. 4

    Last year the student council washed 34 cars and raised $408. How much did they charge per car?

    1. A$12
    2. B$374
    3. C$34
    4. D$10

    Divide the amount raised by the number of cars: 408 ÷ 34 = 12 dollars per car.

    Watch for: Adding the rate instead of multiplying — writing 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 cars for an 8-hour shift instead of 2.5 × 8 = 20. The rate applies to EVERY hour, so the total is always rate × amount.

    1. AAbout 17 days
    2. B400 days
    3. C50 days
    4. D4 days

    Divide the hours by the hours in a day: 400 ÷ 24 ≈ 16.7, which is about 17 days — about 2.5 weeks.

    Watch for: Adding the rate instead of multiplying — writing 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 cars for an 8-hour shift instead of 2.5 × 8 = 20. The rate applies to EVERY hour, so the total is always rate × amount.

    The goal is $500 at $12 per car. Using 12n = 500, at least how many cars must be washed?

    1. A42 cars
    2. B41 cars
    3. C40 cars
    4. D50 cars

    500 ÷ 12 is about 41.7, and you cannot wash a fraction of a car, so round UP: 42 cars raise 42 × 12 = $504, which meets the goal.

    Watch for: Adding the rate instead of multiplying — writing 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 cars for an 8-hour shift instead of 2.5 × 8 = 20. The rate applies to EVERY hour, so the total is always rate × amount.

    4. DNo — 41 cars raise $412

    41 × 12 = 492, and $492 < $500, so 41 cars fall $8 short.

  1. 1

    Suppose the variable s counts 8-hour SHIFTS instead of hours. Which equation gives the cars produced?

    1. Ac = 20s
    2. Bc = 2.5s
    3. Cc = 8s
    4. Dc = 10.5s

    One shift is 8 hours at 2.5 cars per hour, so each shift produces 2.5 × 8 = 20 cars — the rate per shift is 20.

    Watch for: Adding the rate instead of multiplying — writing 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 cars for an 8-hour shift instead of 2.5 × 8 = 20. The rate applies to EVERY hour, so the total is always rate × amount.

  2. 2

    If the plant runs 24 hours a day, 7 days a week for the full 3 weeks, how many hours is that — and can it fill the 1,000-car order?

    1. A504 hours — yes, because 504 hours is more than the 400 hours needed
    2. B168 hours — no, that is less than 400 hours
    3. C72 hours — no, that is less than 400 hours
    4. D504 hours — no, because 504 is less than 1,000

    3 weeks × 7 days × 24 hours = 504 hours, and the order needs only 400 hours, so the plant can fill it with time to spare.

    Watch for: Adding the rate instead of multiplying — writing 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 cars for an 8-hour shift instead of 2.5 × 8 = 20. The rate applies to EVERY hour, so the total is always rate × amount.

  3. 3

    The solution to one problem was 41.7 cars. Would producing or washing a PARTIAL car make sense?

    1. ANo — cars come in whole numbers, so round up to the next whole car
    2. BYes — 0.7 of a car is a real amount you can wash
    3. CNo — so round down and accept a smaller total
    4. DYes — the equation is wrong if it gives a decimal

    You cannot wash seven-tenths of a car, so the model's decimal answer must be interpreted: round up so the goal is still met.

    Watch for: Adding the rate instead of multiplying — writing 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 cars for an 8-hour shift instead of 2.5 × 8 = 20. The rate applies to EVERY hour, so the total is always rate × amount.

  4. 4

    Would you recommend the student council charge $12 or $14.30 per car? Which reasoning is strongest?

    1. AEither can work: $12 needs at least 42 cars washed, while $14.30 needs only about 35 — so the choice depends on how many customers they expect
    2. B$12 is always better because a smaller number is always better
    3. C$14.30 is always better because more money per car is always better
    4. DNeither price can reach the $500 goal

    Both plans reach $500 — 42 × 12 = $504 and 35 × 14.30 = $500.50 — so the real question is whether they can attract 42 cars at the lower price, or whether the higher price might turn customers away.

    Watch for: Adding the rate instead of multiplying — writing 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 cars for an 8-hour shift instead of 2.5 × 8 = 20. The rate applies to EVERY hour, so the total is always rate × amount.
