Where we left off

Our goal: I can describe the ways we are all doers of math, and compare my math story with a classmate's, explain how I can tell, and judge a case I have not seen before.

The big idea: Everyone uses math daily. We do it differently, and sharing how we do it makes all of us better at it — and you can say why it is true, and where it would stop being true.

Model to copy — Watch me estimate the Ferris wheel

  1. The Ferris wheel has 20 cars, and about 4 people fit in each car. About how many people can ride at one time?
  2. I count the cars: 20. Then I estimate how many riders fit in one car: about 4.
  3. I do not count every person — an estimate is a reasoned answer that is close enough to be useful.
  4. 20 × 4 = 80, so I would say “about 80 people.”

  1. 5

    Think it throughThe lesson says, “What we believe about ourselves, about others, and about what we are learning shapes us.” Why does the Strengths Inventory ask you to name a growth area, not only a strength?

    1. ANaming what you want to grow shapes how you approach learning all year, just as naming a strength does
    2. BGrowth areas are only for students who are behind in math
    3. CThe growth area replaces the strength, since you can only claim one
    4. DNaming a growth area has nothing to do with your beliefs about yourself

    Why is that the answer?

    A strength is a belief about what you can do now. A growth area is a belief about what you could do next. Both of them shape how you learn. That is why the lesson asks for both, not one instead of the other.

  2. 6

    Think it throughOne student's math story says she liked math more once she started drawing problems; another's says he liked it more once he memorized steps. What is the point of hearing a classmate's story that is DIFFERENT from yours?

    1. AIt shows there is more than one valid way to be a doer of math, and you might borrow a new strategy
    2. BWhichever method is faster is the “right” one, so you should switch to it
    3. CIf a classmate's story differs from yours, one of you must be doing math wrong
    4. DIt doesn't matter — you only need to hear stories similar to your own

    How do you know? Give a second reason as well.

    The summary says we all do math differently. Learning about each other's strengths helps everyone grow. So a difference between two stories is the point. It is not a sign that either person is wrong.

  3. 7

    Think it throughWhich of these is the BEST example of a specific math strength for the Connect prompt?

    1. A“I can explain my thinking out loud so others can follow my steps”
    2. B“I'm good at math”
    3. C“I got an A last year”
    4. D“Math is easy for me”

    Explain your thinking. What would have to change for a different choice to be right?

    A complete answer names a SPECIFIC strength. “I'm good at math” is a vague self-assessment, not a strength. A grade is a result, not a strength. Name the thing you can actually do.

  4. 8

    Back to the modelThe Ferris wheel has 20 cars, and you estimated about 4 riders fit in each car. Why is 20 × 4 an estimate and not an exact count?

    The book estimates about 80 riders by multiplying 20 cars by about 4 riders per car, without counting every person.

Explain the thinking

  1. 9

    Find the mistakeAnother student made this exact mistake on this lesson. Explain why it is wrong, then write what they should have done.

    The mistake: Believing that 'doing math' only counts when it happens on paper in a classroom.

    The full note above is the teacher's — the student sheet shows only the misconception, not the correction. A correct answer names the step that went wrong, states the rule it broke, and shows the fixed work.

    Watch for: a student who repeats the mistake back without naming why it is wrong.
