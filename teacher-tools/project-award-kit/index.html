Community Math Studio Teacher Kit

Launch, facilitate, assess, and improve the culminating-project modeling cycle.

Open curriculum Evidence framework

Ten-minute launch

  1. Choose the unit project and preview its mathematics target, language target, possible client, and transfer task.
  2. Select a real or role-based audience: facilities staff, family council, library, club, community partner, or peer panel.
  3. Clarify the non-negotiable mathematics and the choices students control.
  4. Schedule draft critique before the final submission. Do not wait until the work is finished.
  5. Decide how students will share: conference, printed gallery, moderated class showcase, or short defense.

Facilitation look-fors

Mathematics stays central

Students explain quantities, units, relationships, assumptions, representations, and tradeoffs—not only decorate a product.

Supports preserve rigor

Visuals, word banks, rehearsal, and frames expand participation while every student tackles the same important mathematics.

Talk is interdependent

Partners must interpret, question, compare, and improve ideas; they do not simply split the workload.

Feedback changes the model

A revision memo names a mathematical change and its reason. Cosmetic editing alone does not satisfy the cycle.

Representations connect

Students explain how an equation relates to a table, graph, diagram, measurement, prototype, or digital model.

Transfer is independent

The final changed case reveals whether students can adapt the underlying relationship without prompts from the original interface.

WIDA-aligned discourse routine

  1. Private think time: estimate, sketch, annotate, or rehearse in any language.
  2. Explain: describe the representation, quantities, and strategy.
  3. Argue: state a claim, cite mathematical evidence, and connect the evidence to the claim.
  4. Discuss: ask for clarification, revoice, compare, agree or disagree with evidence, and revise.
  5. Bridge: choose language and representations appropriate for the final audience.

Scoring and calibration

  • Score content accuracy, modeling/reasoning, language development, and revision as separate dimensions.
  • Do not reduce a mathematics score because a multilingual learner uses emerging English.
  • Collect three anchor examples for each rubric level and annotate the evidence that justifies the score.
  • Double-score at least 20% of a pilot sample; discuss disagreements and revise ambiguous descriptors.
  • Include an individual transfer response even when the project product is collaborative.

Low-tech and recovery path

Students can complete the same cycle on paper using seven labeled sections. Calculations, diagrams, prototype photos, and critique notes can be attached to a printed report. The assessment constructs remain the same: define, assume, model twice, validate, critique, revise, defend, and transfer.
