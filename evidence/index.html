Evidence before claims

Community Math Studio

A Grade 6 culminating-project model in which students define a consequential problem, compare mathematical models, test assumptions, reason across languages and representations, revise after critique, and defend a decision.

Learning model Measures Current status Open curriculum

Current evidence status

The shared Community Math Studio learning contract is implemented across the 22 standard culminating projects. Outcome collection begins with classroom use. No causal learning, equity, or scale claim is published before common measures, scoring calibration, and implementation records are available.

The learning model

Discover + define

Identify a stakeholder, need, constraints, and success criteria.

Estimate + assume

Estimate before calculating and name quantities, units, assumptions, and unknowns.

Build two models

Connect equations with a table, diagram, graph, prototype, or digital representation.

Test + validate

Use evidence, measurements, extreme cases, or another representation to test reasonableness.

Critique + revise

Respond to a peer's notice, question, and challenge with a mathematical revision.

Defend + transfer

Defend the decision, acknowledge a limitation, respond to an audience, and solve a novel case.

What students are expected to demonstrate

Mathematical substance

Accurate content, connected representations, assumptions, validation, tradeoffs, and transfer.

Mathematical language

WIDA-aligned explaining and arguing using quantities, representations, evidence, and respectful critique.

Agency

Meaningful choice of stakeholder, context, model, evidence, language resources, and final recommendation.

Improvement

Visible movement from estimate and first model through feedback, revision, defense, and a new case.

What counts as evidence of learning

Finishing an activity and understanding it are not the same thing, so they are recorded separately. These five states are what a teacher sees — they are descriptions of a moment, not labels for a student.

State What it means What it does not mean
Completion The work was opened and attempted. Nothing about whether it was understood.
Supported performance Correct, with a hint, a visual, or a conversation along the way. Not a lesser result — it is the expected middle of learning.
Independent mastery Correct on a first attempt with no hint and no struggle signal. Not proof it will still be there next week.
Transfer Solved a problem in a context the lesson never showed. Not the same as repeating a practised procedure.
Retention Still correct several days later, unprompted. Not measured until enough days have passed.

Where there is not enough evidence to say, the teacher view says “insufficient evidence” rather than showing a zero.

What is stored, and where

  • On this device. Your work in progress, your save code, and a short queue of learning signals are kept in this browser’s local storage. Clearing your browser data removes them.
  • Sent to the school’s own server. Learning signals — which question was attempted, whether it was right, whether a hint was opened, how long a page was open — are sent to EduWonderLab’s own database so a teacher can see what the class found hard. This information does leave the device.
  • Not collected. No advertising identifiers, no location, no contact details, and no third-party analytics. Nothing is sent to any outside company.
  • Written explanations. What a student types as an explanation is saved with their work so they can come back to it. It is not fed into the class summary counts.

How save codes work

A save code is a short code that stands in for your work — not for you. It lets you pick up on another device, and it lets a teacher find a piece of work without a full name attached to it. Keep it like a locker number: it is not secret, but it is yours.

How teachers use this

To decide what to teach next — which misconception to address, who to pull into a small group, and who is ready for something harder. Recommendations always show which signals produced them, so a teacher can disagree with the suggestion.

For families

You do not need to teach the lesson, and you do not need to know the maths. The most useful thing you can do takes about five minutes: ask what they tried, listen to the explanation, and encourage another attempt.

  • Ask — “what did you try first?”
  • Listen — let them explain it out loud, even imperfectly.
  • Encourage — getting it wrong once and revising is the point.

Family activities are optional and are never graded. Nothing is recorded about whether a family took part.

Limits of AI-generated support

Some activities offer an AI study helper. It is there to ask what you tried and give the smallest useful hint — not to give answers.

  • It can be wrong. Always check its maths against your own work or ask your teacher.
  • Your teacher decides whether it is used, and every AI task has a non-AI alternative.
  • Do not type private information — no full name, address, phone number, grades, or anything about your health or home life. Only type about the maths problem.
  • If you are stuck or upset, ask your teacher. A person is better than a chatbot.

Clear this device’s learning data

This removes saved work, save codes, and any queued learning signals stored in this browser. It cannot remove signals that were already sent to the teacher’s database — ask your teacher for that.

Reporting commitments

  • Publish sample size, implementation dates, dosage, missing evidence, and limitations.
  • Report effect magnitude and uncertainty rather than relying only on testimonials.
  • Keep null or negative findings visible and document what changed because of them.
  • Separate interface completion from demonstrated mathematics and transfer.
  • Version the rubric, project contract, and implementation guide.
