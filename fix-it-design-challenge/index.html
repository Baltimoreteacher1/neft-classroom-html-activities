Fix-It Design Challenge

A teacher needs a prompt. Your group writes it.

What happened: a teacher asked AI for help making a task reachable. What came back did the students' thinking for them.

What you do: write the AI prompt for them — by choosing one option in each of four rows.

What you turn in: that prompt, posted to Padlet. The page writes the post — you tap Copy.

Why: a well-worded prompt is the difference between AI opening a task up and AI doing it for students. The four pieces you pick today are the four pieces every good request needs — reusable all year.

1

Four short steps. About 15 minutes.

  1. Read the problem. Your scenario is already written.
  2. Write the prompt. Four rows, one tap each.
  3. Rate it. Pick an OSAMR level, finish one sentence.
  4. Post it. Tap Copy, paste into Padlet.

What you produce: one rewritten prompt, posted to Padlet beside the broken one. The page writes the post — you just tap Copy.

Everything else is written for you. The only thing your group writes is the new prompt.

2

The OSAMR framework

This is how we name what AI did. The goal is not the highest letter — it is the honest one.

S

Substitution

Same task. AI replaces a tool.

A

Augmentation

Same task. AI adds useful support.

M

Modification

Task is redesigned, but the target stays intact.

R

Redefinition

AI enables a new learning move students could not easily do before.

O

Overreliance

AI does the thinking, answer, evidence, or product for students.

3

Watch one group do it

Here is a real case, finished. Same four steps you are about to do, with a Grade 7 ELA group's answers already filled in.

Step 1 of 4

Read the problem

They read what the teacher sent and what came back.

The prompt they were fixing

“Summarize this article for 7th graders and tell students the author's central claim and two pieces of evidence they should use in their response.”

What came back: the AI named the claim and picked the evidence. Students could answer from the summary without ever reading the article.

The test their new prompt had to pass

  • AI may help with dense text and close-reading load.
  • AI must never name the claim or choose the evidence.
  • Students still identify the claim, select evidence, and explain the connection.

Step 2 of 4

Rewrite the teacher's prompt

They loaded the real request, deleted the line that gave the answer away (“tell students the author's central claim and two pieces of evidence”), then tapped chips to add what AI should build and what it must never do.

Their rewritten prompt

Create access supports for 7th graders reading this article.
• Chunk the text with headings.
• Include a vocabulary bank with words only.
• Add one guiding question per part.
• Include a blank organizer for students to fill in.
• Do not summarize or replace the text.
• Do not choose the evidence for students.
• Do not explain the reasoning.

Step 3 of 4

Rate your new prompt

One level, one sentence.

OSAMR level

Augmentation

Students still have to…

Students still have to identify the claim, select their own evidence, and explain how it supports the claim.

Step 4 of 4

Post it to Padlet

The page wrote this for them. They ticked the box and tapped Copy.

Their Padlet post

Group — ELA Evidence

Problem AI Input:
"Summarize this article for 7th graders and tell students the author's
central claim and two pieces of evidence they should use."

Our fixed AI prompt:
Create access supports for 7th graders reading this article.
• Chunk the text with headings.
• Include a vocabulary bank with words only.
• Do not choose the evidence for students.

OSAMR rating:
Augmentation

Rigor evidence:
Students still have to identify the claim, select their own evidence,
and explain how it supports the claim.
The one rule: ask AI for supports — vocabulary, questions, structure, frames, checklists. Never ask it for the answer, the claim, the evidence, the reasoning, or a filled-in organizer.
